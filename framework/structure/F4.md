**One-line result (decided).** Divide F4 by the role the object plays in the practice (six sub-families: sacred focus and images, personal devotion aids, rite vessels and implements, light and incense and offerings, divination tools, and the dead), closed by the parts sub-family F4.7 (CV-17, F-19), then by a leaf axis stated for each sub-family, then by the **generic form** a person owns (kind), then by named form. **Tradition is a modifier and a generated view, not a level.** Under C11 a tradition's own name for a form that a generic kind already covers is a **named form** under that kind with a required tradition tag (rosary, tasbih, mala and juzu under *prayer beads*; butsudan and kamidana under *household altar or shrine*; koro under *incense burner*; rin and singing bowl under *standing bowl bell*); a tradition term becomes the name of a kind only where no generic kind covers its form (mezuzah scroll, tefillin, seder plate, monstrance). Occasion, material, size, user (adult, child, pet), setting (home, congregation) and price are tags or attributes. Kinds stop where buyers compare quality or makers. No tier sits on a kind: steel gauge and wood are attributes, relic class is a classification attribute, and the kosher scroll grade enters on one fetched source as a row of the C16 grading-scale registry, with no grade value stored per scroll (F-19). Makers are incidental to the buyer's choice, but every attested maker (Judaica silversmiths, church-candle makers, urn design houses, tarot publishers and the rest) is recorded in the shared domain maker ledger, uncapped (F-20).

## 0. Category

**Code and name.** F4 Religious & Ceremonial Objects (domain F, Culture, Records & Meaning).

**Membership test (placement key, step 14).** "Made for **use in religious or spiritual practice, or to hold, mark or accompany the dead**: altars, shrines, icons, prayer beads, tefillin, ritual vessels, lamps, instruments and implements, incense and incense burners made for a rite, religious festival objects used in worship (menorah, diya, Advent wreath, home nativity set), tarot and oracle decks, coffins and caskets, cremation urns for people or pets, grave markers." Category line: "objects for religious practice and for the dead, worn or not, except dress and ornaments."

**Notes and rules that already touch F4.**
- Step 14 note: objects for weddings, christenings, graduations and other occasions are filed as what they are, so occasion is a modifier. Religious dress and vestments are A1. Anything worn as an ornament, including amulets worn as pendants, rings or bracelets, is A3. Decorations for any festival, religious or secular (Christmas trees and ornaments, Easter, Diwali, Halloween, Hina-matsuri displays) are B4 unless made for use in worship; a festival basket is a basket (B1). Home-fragrance incense is B4. Instruments also used outside worship, such as an organ or harmonium, are E3. Card and board games are E1. Meditation cushions and mats are B2. In CATEGORIES_v0_7 the step 14 note also carries the decided sentences on devotional medals (K-107), devotional images (K-108), card-play tarot packs (K-109), singing bowls (K-110), prayer strands (K-111), texts, scrolls and prayer cards (K-112), candles and prayer rugs (K-113), divination tools (K-114), fraternal regalia and smudge bundles (K-115), Japanese stone lanterns (K-116) and carried charms (K-117).
- Step 7 note (F6)
: religious relics and rudraksha beads are F4, not specimens. Step 13 note (B10): pet cremation urns and memorials are F4. Step 4 (G2): a burial plot or niche is property; step 4 sends buildings to G2, so a mausoleum is G2 and a grave marker is F4.
- Step 15 (F3): medals, decorations and regalia of office are F3; step 14 is tried first. Step 20 (F2): pictures and free-standing sculpture are F2, but step 14 is tried first. Step 21 (F1): books "including religious texts" are F1. Step 19 (F5): issued documents and keepsake papers. Step 3: groceries (communion wafers, kosher wine and matzah are food or drink, not F4).
- Appendix A modifiers that bite here: region and culture, era, price band and quality tier, brand and maker, scene and subculture, user (including pet versus person), setting, installation, power source and technology, occasion (wedding, festival, season), motive (heirloom, memorabilia), material, condition, edition.

**Gaps in the key that this research hit (details in section 9; all are now closed by sentences in the step 14 note of CATEGORIES_v0_7, K-107 to K-117, and by B4-O2 and F-17).** Prayer rugs (B2 mats versus F4), singing bowls (E3 versus F4), candles made for a rite (named by neither B3 nor step 14), devotional paintings such as icons, thangkas and altarpieces (F2 versus F4, where the antiques trade and the auction trade treat them as pictures), religious medals (F3, A3 or F4), Masonic and other fraternal ritual objects (a ritual but not obviously a religion), sage and other smudge bundles (B9 or B4 or F4), memorial jewellery holding ashes (A3 by the ornament rule), spirit boards (sold as board games, used for divination) and tarot packs sold for card play (E1 or F4).

### 1.7 Where the sources agree and where they split

- **Agree.** Every neutral standard (AAT, Nomenclature, Google, UNSPSC) classes these objects by what they are for (worship, devotion, funeral, wedding), and puts **no religious tradition above the object**. Single-tradition shops, whose whole shop is the tradition, divide by object (Judaica.com, HECK, DharmaShop, Hasegawa). Funeral shops divide by object, material and user, not religion (S20, S21, S26, S27).
- **Split.** (1) Occasion versus role: Nomenclature makes Funerary, Religious, Holiday and Wedding sibling subclasses (a purpose axis); AAT makes religious objects divide into cult, devotional, liturgical and relics (a role axis). (2) Tradition: the antiques market (S22) and curated museum departments (S09, S37) name the tradition first, but the museum that holds Judaica as a collecting area (S37) physically displays it by form. (3) Funerary: Nomenclature separates it from "formal religious worship"; Google lists it beside religion; F4 joins them. (4) Pets: funeral shops run a parallel pet tree (S27) while F4's own note files pet urns here.

## 2. The first axis people choose by

**What owners and buyers decide first.** Four decisions come in this order, but the first is usually settled before any category tree is opened.

1. **Which practice the object is for (tradition).** Buyers settle this by choosing where to shop, not by clicking a category: Judaica.com, The Catholic Shop, HECK, Muslim Mall, DharmaShop, Hasegawa and Sri Diya are each one tradition (or two neighbouring ones, as in Japan) and none has a tradition level in its own menu (S13, S19, S25, S28, S29, S16, S12). In shops that serve every practice at once, the tradition becomes a navigation level only in the antiques trade (S22) and in curated museum departments (S09).
2. **What the object does in the rite.** Inside a single-tradition shop this is the top menu: tallit, tefillin, mezuzah, kiddush cup, shofar, candlesticks (S13); chalice, monstrance, censer, eternal light (S25); butsudan, ihai, kamidana, butsugu, incense (S16, S17); singing bowls, prayer flags, malas, incense, statues (S29). In every neutral standard it is also the top split (S01, S02, S04, S05, S10, S11).
3. **Occasion, where one exists.** Occasion is a menu in its own right in three shops (Judaica.com Wedding, High Holidays, Kids and lifecycle; The Catholic Shop Baptism, Confirmation, First Holy Communion, Wedding, Christmas; HECK occasion candles) (S13, S19, S25). The framework already makes occasion a modifier (step 14 note), and the evidence supports a tag: the same Hanukkah lamp is a Hanukkah object and a candle holder.
4. **Material, size and price.** Material is the first navigational filter for urns (Memorials.com: wood 2,139, ceramic 1,769, metal 1,621, marble 1,053; House of Urns: powder-coated steel, stainless steel, brass, bronze, ceramic) and for coffins (sarg-urne.de by wood) (S20, S21, S26), a sub-menu under the object in Judaica.com (S13), a top-level axis in Sri Diya (S12) and the tasbih (S28). Size matters when parts must fit: a mezuzah scroll comes in four sizes and "it is important for the scroll to fit comfortably into the case" (S32).

**What the questions look like.** For funeral goods the question is about the person or pet and the plan for the ashes: House of Urns opens with "Select pet or person" and an Urn Finder (S21); Memorials.com lists keepsake, companion, scattering, biodegradable and urn vault as separate categories (S20); casket guides weigh steel gauge against hardwood and warn that sealing adds cost without benefit (S33). For ritual goods the question is what the rite needs and for whom: Japanese altar pages sort altars by style (modern, traditional karaki, traditional gold-leaf, compact, wall-hung) and offer price bands (S16); the e-commerce data show buyers split by purpose, with incense, candles and beads at the low band and altars carrying 56.6 percent of sales (S18).

**Conclusion for the axis.** The first axis a buyer *uses* is the tradition (resolved by venue) and then the object's role in the practice. The first axis a *neutral* catalogue can use is the role, because a neutral catalogue has to hold all practices at once. The role axis also survives where tradition does not apply: tarot and oracle decks (S24, S10), neopagan tools (S30), funeral goods (S20, S27) and secular memorial objects.

## 3. Candidate structures

### 3.0 The question: is tradition a modifier view or a first axis?

Evidence for **modifier view** (recommended):
1. **Every neutral museum and trade standard splits by function, never tradition.** AAT places ceremonial objects under object genres by function, with cult, devotional, liturgical and relic children (S01, S02); Nomenclature uses Religious, Funerary, Wedding, Holiday subclasses (S03, S04, S05); Google has Religious Items, Memorial Ceremony Supplies and Wedding Ceremony Supplies (S10); UNSPSC lists commodities by object (S11). Tradition appears in none of those trees as a level; it appears only inside term names (rosary, seder plate, vajra, tankas).
2. **Museums that hold a tradition as a collecting area still catalogue the object by type.** The Rubin lists "ritual objects" as a classification inside a Himalayan museum (S07); the Israel Museum has a Jewish Art department and the subject "Hanukkah lamp" (S09); the MFA defines Judaica by tradition yet hangs a Hanukkah lamp among European decorative arts and puts the shofar in the Musical Instruments Gallery (S37). Nomenclature itself (S06) requires cross-indexing because one object has several legitimate parents.
3. **One function recurs across traditions.** Prayer beads are the rosary (S19), tasbih (S28), mala (S29) and juzu (S16); the ritual lamp is the menorah (S01, S09), the eternal light (S25), the butter-lamp holder (S34) and the lamp in a Hindu pooja shop (S12); the household shrine is the butsudan, the kamidana (S16, S17) and the home temple (S15); the incense burner is the censer (S25), the koro (S16, S17) and the UNSPSC commodity (S11); the ritual bell is the altar bell (S04), the ghanta (S01), the singing bowl (S29) and the rin (S16). A tradition-first tree would repeat each of these leaves five or six times, and (C11) a tree that keeps each tradition's name as a sibling kind beside the generic kind does the same thing one level lower.
4. **Objects belong to more than one tradition, and to practices that are not religions.** A Buddhist shop sells rudraksha malas (S29); a Japanese shop holds Buddhist and Shinto goods side by side (S16, S17); the Proantic split has to add Masonic Art and Other Religions & Rituals (S22); tarot and neopagan tools have no church (S24, S30); funeral goods are not tied to a faith (S20, S26).
5. **A tradition-first tree is lopsided.** In the one source that does it, Christian Art is 5,054 of 5,811 objects and Judaica 58 (S22). A top-level split with that skew gives owners of Islamic or Buddhist goods almost no structure and cannot hold the long tail.
6. **The framework already rules it out.** Appendix A lists "region and culture" and "scene and subculture" as modifiers, and rule S4 forbids modifiers splitting the top level; C11 applies the same logic inside the category ("a region never names a sub-family or a leaf").

Evidence for a **first axis** (and why it does not win):
1. Dealers, collectors and single-tradition museums navigate by tradition (S22, S23, S09). *Answer:* that is a **view**, and the framework allows generated views. One tag per kind and named form gives the Proantic user "Judaica", "Islamic art" and "Other religions" for free.
2. Some objects are only meaningful inside one tradition and have tradition names (butsudan, kamidana, mezuzah, tefillin, tankas, ihai). *Answer (C11):* where a generic kind already covers the form, the tradition name is a **named form** under it with a required tradition tag (butsudan and kamidana under *household altar or shrine*; thangka under *devotional scroll painting*; ihai under *memorial tablet*). Only where no generic kind covers the form does the tradition term name a kind, with a generic description beside it (*mezuzah scroll*, *tefillin*, *seder plate*, *monstrance*). Tradition is then derived from tags, not a level.
3. Within a tradition, sects change the object: a Sufi-order tasbih (Tijani, Jerrahi, Naqshbandi) (S28); the Chabad script of a mezuzah scroll (S32); a Shinto soreisha altar rather than a Buddhist butsudan (S16). *Answer:* a sect form that is itself a whole altar or object type is a named form of the kind (soreisha); a sect difference inside a named form is a C3 variant (Sufi-order tasbihs, `variant_type` style) or an attribute (mezuzah `script_style`), because a named form cannot carry named forms.

**Recommendation on the question (decided, F-3):** tradition is a **multi-valued tag and a generated view** attached to kinds and named forms (several values allowed: rudraksha malas carry Hindu and Buddhist), with a required value on every named form that carries a tradition name. It is never a level, and no tradition name is kept as a C2(c) exception kind (F-3; the CV-27 two-region test decides level only, and the butsudan is sold as its own category in Japan only, S16, S17). The same holds for occasion (Hanukkah, Diwali, Advent, Obon), life-cycle stage (birth, wedding, death) and setting (home, congregation, institution).

### 3.1 Candidate A — role in the practice first (recommended)

- **Levels and axes.** Sub-family: *the role the object plays for the practitioner* (six values). Leaf: *one axis stated per sub-family* (C5(c); listed in 3.4). Kind: *generic form a person can own* (C11). Named form: *tradition form, style or iconic design* (`maker_bound` yes or no, C2).
- **Sub-families.** F4.1 Altars, shrines and sacred images (what is set up to be venerated). F4.2 Personal devotion aids (what the practitioner holds, counts, kneels on or carries to pray). F4.3 Ritual vessels, implements and instruments (what is handled in the rite). F4.4 Light, incense and offerings (what is lit, burned or set out). F4.5 Divination and esoteric tools (what is read or cast). F4.6 The dead (what holds the remains, marks the dead, or covers the body or coffin). F4.7 Parts and accessories made for one religious or funerary object (CV-17; opened now by F-19; leaf axis = object served).
- **Precedence inside F4.** Test in this order: the dead (holds remains, marks the dead, covers the body or coffin), divination, light incense and offerings (lit, burned or set out), sacred focus, rite implement, personal aid. So a memorial lamp that is lit in a rite stays a ritual lamp (F4.4) with a memorial tag, a grave light is a ritual candle (F4.4), an ihai on a butsudan marks the dead (F4.6), and an urn that holds ashes is F4.6 whatever its decoration.
- **Examples (14 real kinds).** household altar or shrine (S10, S16), devotional statue (S11, S19), devotional scroll painting (thangka S29, kakejiku S17), prayer beads (S10), prayer rug (S28), ritual cup (chalice S25, kiddush cup S13), mezuzah scroll (S13, S32), shofar (S13), Hanukkah lamp (S09), incense burner (S11), swung censer (S25), tarot deck (S10), cremation urn (S10), grave marker (S05).
- **Where it breaks.** (1) A Hanukkah lamp is both a ritual lamp (F4.4) and a festival object; handled by the occasion tag. (2) Tefillin and a mezuzah are worn or fixed objects with a text inside; handled by the F4.3 leaf "text-bearing objects", with the scroll and its case as separate kinds. (3) A sanctuary lamp and an altar candle could be F4.1 (sacred furniture) or F4.4; rule: lit objects are F4.4. (4) A pet urn: same F4.6 kind with a user tag, as step 13's note directs.

### 3.2 Candidate B — occasion or purpose first (Nomenclature style)

- **Levels and axes.** Subclass: *the occasion or purpose the object serves* (religious worship, funerary, wedding, holiday, party, organizational) (S03). Then object term (S04, S05).
- **Examples (12).** altar, altar bell, prayer wheel, religious lamp (S04); burial shroud, coffin plate, funeral pall, tombstone (S05); breaking glass, ring pillow, marriage bowl (S01, S10); Advent wreath, menorah (framework step 14).
- **Where it breaks.** (1) A Hanukkah lamp is religious and holiday at once. (2) A Yahrzeit candle is a ritual lamp used in mourning. (3) A christening plate or a first-communion ribbon are occasion objects, but step 14's note says occasion is a modifier, so the framework cannot file by it. (4) The Nomenclature guide admits the problem and works around it by cross-indexing (S06); this taxonomy has one home per object, so it cannot.

### 3.3 Candidate C — tradition first (Proantic style)

- **Levels and axes.** Level 1: *the religion or practice* (Christian, Islamic, Jewish, Buddhist, Hindu, Shinto, neopagan, Masonic, none). Level 2: object family. Level 3: kind (S22 offers only the first level).
- **Examples (12).** Judaism: mezuzah scroll (S32), kiddush cup (S13), Hanukkah lamp (S09); Christianity: crucifix (S19), monstrance (S25), rosary (S19); Islam: tasbih, prayer rug (S28); Buddhism: butsudan, juzu (S16), mala (S29); neopagan: athame (S30).
- **Where it breaks.** (1) Prayer beads and incense burners recur in every branch and would be stored five times; the one-home rule needs a single parent. (2) Rudraksha malas in a Buddhist shop belong to two traditions (S29). (3) Tarot decks have no tradition (S10, S24); secular memorial goods have none either (S20). (4) The skew in S22 (87 percent Christian) leaves most branches almost empty. (5) C11: a region or tradition never names a sub-family or a leaf.

## 4. Depth and size

These are estimates from the shape of the sources; nothing was counted item by item.

- **Generic kinds.** Roughly **120 to 200**. Anchors: the AAT religious and ceremonial branches list about 250 object genres (S01, S02), of which texts (F1), ceremonial costume (A1), ceremonial weapons (E4 or F3) and a few buildings fall outside F4, and many are tradition terms for one form; Nomenclature's Funerary subclass shows about 15 terms and its Religious subclass at least 40 (the fetched page truncated the list) (S04, S05); the Japanese altar-fittings tree has about 15 butsugu kinds plus about 10 kamidana fittings (S16, S17); Hindu pooja, Catholic church supply and Judaica shops each list 20 to 40 distinct object types (S12, S25, S13). The earlier estimate (220 to 320) counted tradition names as kinds; under C11 most of them (perhaps 30 to 40 percent of the earlier total) become named forms of a generic kind.
- **Named forms.** No cap (BRIEF depth-and-breadth rule; CV-7): named forms are harvested in stage 3 from existing catalogues first and grow as far as the attested evidence goes. Estimate, not a limit: roughly **800 to 1,800 non-maker named forms** (tradition forms of each generic kind: household altars, prayer beads, bells, lamps, burners, scroll paintings; prayer rug types, six in S02; ritual candle forms, S25; urn and casket forms; Marseille and other generic deck patterns), plus **maker-bound named forms** in the thousands: published deck designs (Aeclectic alone lists about 2,000 decks, S24) and the urn and casket designs of design houses and makers (S20, S27). Butsudan styles, bead materials, Sufi-order tasbihs and mezuzah script styles stay attributes or C3 variants, not named forms (C2(b)).
- **Where depth concentrates.** (1) Rite vessels and implements, because every tradition has a set (Catholic liturgical set S25; Judaica S13; Buddhist and Shinto butsugu and shingu S16, S17). (2) Light and incense. (3) The dead, where funeral shops carry hundreds of urn designs but few kinds (S20, S26, S27).
- **Breadth by leaf.** Broad (15 or more kinds when filled): vessels; hand implements; lamps and candles; three-dimensional images. Medium (5 to 15): altars and shrines; sound-makers; text-bearing objects; incense and burners; other divination tools. Narrow (under 5): relics and reliquaries; counted aids (one kind, prayer beads, with many named forms); offering vessels; coffins; urns; markers; each F4.7 parts leaf.
- **Depth.** Four levels below the category at most (sub-family, leaf, kind, named form; C5(a)). A sub-family that holds about 12 kinds or fewer may skip the leaf level (C5(b)); F4.2, F4.5, F4.6 and F4.7 qualify, and if their leaves are dropped the kinds are stored with `leaf = null`, never under a dummy leaf. The memo keeps their leaves because each states an axis the review asked for (F4-2); F4.7 keeps its object-served leaves as CV-17 states the axis.

## 4b. Choice level

**A kind stops at the level where buyers actually compare quality or makers.** Evidence, sub-family by sub-family:

- **Mezuzah and tefillin (F4.3).** The scroll and the case are compared separately: cases by style and material (Judaica.com sub-menus by glass, metal, wood, ceramic, S13), scrolls by size class and **kosher grade** (aJudaica: "all aJudaica mezuzah parchments are 100% kosher. The terms: basic, upgraded and mehudar refer to graded levels of excellence", S32). So *mezuzah case* and *mezuzah scroll* are two kinds; script style (Ashkenazi, Sephardic, Ari, Chabad) is an attribute; the grade is one row of the C16 grading-scale registry, entered on this one fetched source, with no grade value stored per scroll (F-19; section 5).
- **Cremation urns (F4.6).** The funeral shops split by capacity or purpose first and material second: keepsake, companion, scattering, biodegradable, urn vault (S20), with material as the filter (S20, S21, S27). Keepsake, companion and full urns differ by how much they hold, so they are one kind (*cremation urn*) with `capacity_class` as an attribute (C6). Scattering urn and urn vault differ by form and use and stay kinds. Material, theme and user (adult, child, pet) are attributes or tags; pet urns are the same kinds with a user tag, since the funeral trade runs a parallel tree (S27) and the framework note files pet urns here.
- **Coffins and caskets (F4.6).** Buyers compare by material and by measurable spec: steel gauge, stainless, copper, pine, oak, mahogany, biodegradable, rental (S33). One kind (*coffin or casket*) with material and gauge as attributes; the rental casket is a named form.
- **Altars (F4.1).** Japanese buyers compare altar type (modern, karaki, gold-leaf, compact, wall-hung, soreisha) (S16). The generic form is the household altar or shrine; butsudan, kamidana, soreisha and the home temple are named forms (C11); karaki, gold-leaf and modern are a `style` attribute, wall-hung an installation attribute (C12) and compact a size attribute (C6). No butsudan exception kind (F-3).
- **Prayer beads (F4.2).** Shops make the tradition's own string the unit (rosary S19, tasbih S28, mala S29, juzu S16) and then compare by material (wood, stone, bone, sterling silver, seed and nut S28; bodhi seed, rudraksha, sandalwood S29). The generic form is *prayer beads* (S10, S02), which C11 makes the kind; the four strings are named forms; rudraksha, bodhi seed and sandalwood are C3 `material` variants of the mala (the step 7 note still sends loose rudraksha beads to F4).
- **Statues and images (F4.1).** Retailers split by the figure shown (Mary, Christ, saints S19; deities S15). The *figure* is an iconography tag, so there is one *devotional statue* kind, not one per figure or tradition. Flat images split by form: panel painting (icon) and scroll painting (thangka, kakejiku), because a hanging scroll and a painted panel are made, mounted and kept differently.
- **Bells and drums (F4.3).** A hand bell (altar bell, ghanta), a standing bowl bell (rin, singing bowl) and a slit drum (mokugyo) differ in form and in how they are struck; the tradition names are named forms.
- **Candles and incense (F4.4).** HECK lists altar candles by maker and wax content (S25: "100 percent Ceresin" or "10 percent beeswax"); occasion candles (baptism, communion, Easter) are one kind (*ritual candle*) with an occasion tag, and the Yahrzeit candle is a named form of it. Incense is a kind (*ritual incense*) with a form attribute (stick, cone, resin, church grade); a standing incense burner and a swung censer are two kinds because the swung form differs (S25, S01).
- **Divination (F4.5).** Aeclectic compares decks by theme, publisher and style (S24); the kind stops at *tarot deck*, *oracle deck*, *Lenormand deck*, and each published deck is a maker-bound named form under it (C2(a)).

**The choice level differs between sub-families.** It is deepest in F4.3 (every vessel and implement is a separate generic form because shops list them separately and tradition sets define them) and shallowest in F4.2, F4.5 and in urns (a few kinds, many named forms or attributes).

## 8. Order inside a leaf

**Decided (C7; CV-20, CV-21):** kinds go **canonical generic kind first**, then the other kinds of the leaf alphabetically (the sub-family's stated secondary key; no faith or form is ranked). Under each kind, **non-maker named forms first, by first-attested year, then alphabetically; maker-bound named forms follow**, by maker (ledger `name`), then first-introduction year, then alphabetically. Variants go by `variant_code` or year. The conformance pass grouped non-maker named forms by tradition; CV-21 adopted the stored order without that grouping, so a "by tradition" order is a generated view read from the tradition tags (C2(c)), not stored order.

Reason: AAT and Nomenclature order terms alphabetically inside a parent (S01, S02, S04, S05), a rule that avoids ranking faiths; museums order objects by form and period (the MFA hangs Hanukkah lamps in the 18th-century European gallery, S37); the AAT's own named forms for prayer rugs are typed, not ranked (S02). Retailers order by frequency or by the use-sequence of the rite (Judaica.com runs from Shabbat through the High Holidays, S13), which suits a shop but not a catalogue. Example: *prayer beads* → named forms by first-attested year once the stage-3 rows carry years; until then alphabetically: chaplet, juzu, mala, rosary, tasbih. The generated tradition view shows Catholic (chaplet, rosary), Hindu and Buddhist (mala), Japanese Buddhist (juzu), Muslim (tasbih).

## 9. Boundaries

The closest cross-boundary objects, with precedence sentences. Every boundary is now decided; the decision id is given with each, and the sentences named K-107 to K-117 are in the step 14 note of CATEGORIES_v0_7.

1. **F4 versus A1 Clothing (religious dress).** Kippah, tallit, tzitzit, kufi, vestments, habits and baptism or communion dresses are dress and go to A1; tefillin are named in step 14 and go to F4 although they are strapped to the body. *Precedence: a garment worn as dress is A1, even when only worn in worship; a strapped box, scroll or wrapped ritual object that step 14 names is F4.* A burial shroud wraps the dead and is F4 (kind 62), not dress.
2. **F4 versus A3 Jewellery.** Cross, hamsa, Star of David and mezuzah pendants, rosary bracelets, tasbih bracelets (S28) and ash pendants (S21, S27) are worn ornaments and go to A3. *Precedence: anything worn as an ornament is A3, and a prayer string kept in the hand is F4; a rosary is F4 and a rosary bracelet is A3.* Decided for prayer-bead necklaces and malas (K-111): *"A strand made for counting prayers (with counter, tassel or guru bead) is F4; a necklace of the same beads made as jewellery is A3."* Decided for ashes (F4-O1): jewellery made to be worn that holds cremation ashes is A3; a keepsake urn that is not worn stays F4.6.
3. **F4 versus B4 Decor.** Christmas trees, ornaments, Diwali and Hina-matsuri displays, home-fragrance incense and garden statues are B4. *Precedence: incense or a lamp made for a rite is F4, home-fragrance incense and decorative candles are B4; a statue of a figure made to be venerated is F4, a garden statue is B4.* A nativity set and an Advent wreath are F4 by step 14's list ("used in worship"). Decided for lanterns: *"A Japanese stone lantern (tōrō, ishi-dōrō) is B4 garden decor; it is F4 only when made as a shrine offering, shown by its form (votive inscription and shape)"* (K-116); a lantern made for Obon to welcome the dead (bon-chōchin) is F4 (B4-O2, provisional until attested as memorial goods); any other lantern follows K-180 (made to be carried as a light B3; made to stand or hang as decoration B4), and so does an open candle holder for the home (B4), while one made for a rite (altar candlestick) is F4 because step 14 is tried first. Shimenawa is F4 (kind 63) and kadomatsu B4 (B4-O2).
4. **F4 versus B2 Soft Furnishings (mats and cushions).** Decided (K-113): *"A rug made as a prayer rug is F4; a meditation cushion or mat is B2."* A prayer rug (qibla niche, travel, children's) is kind 11.

Shofar, altar bells, mokugyo, rin and ghanta are made for rites and go to F4. *Precedence: an instrument made for a rite is F4, an instrument also used outside worship is E3.* Decided (K-110): *"Singing bowls and meditation instruments are E3 unless made for a rite (F4)."* A rin made for an altar is F4 (kind 32).
6. **F4 versus F1 and F5.** A published religious text is F1 (step 21); the text inside a mezuzah or tefillin is handwritten and is part of the ritual object (F4); a handwritten Torah scroll used in the rite is F4 (kind 29 †) and a printed Torah or Bible is F1 (F1 adds the same sentence, review F1-10(c)). A ketubah or baptism certificate is a document (F5) or art (F2) by form, not F4. A prayer card is a printed devotional card (S10) and goes to F4 under step 14 as a devotional object. *Decided (K-112): a bound or printed religious text is F1; a scroll or card made to be fixed, worn or used in a rite (Torah scroll, mezuzah, prayer card) is F4; a ketubah or baptism certificate is F5, or F2 when made as a picture.*

The antiques trade lists icons (284) and religious paintings (3,035) under Painting (S22) while AAT lists altarpieces and tankas as religious visual works (S02). Kind 14 †.

## 10. Recommendation (decided)

**Final structure.** F4 is divided by the role the object plays in the practice into six sub-families, F4.1 Altars, shrines and sacred images; F4.2 Personal devotion aids; F4.3 Ritual vessels, implements and instruments; F4.4 Light, incense and offerings; F4.5 Divination and esoteric tools; F4.6 The dead, closed by F4.7 Parts and accessories made for one religious or funerary object (CV-17, opened now by F-19: Torah finial, coffin plate, coffin pillow, urn appliqué or medallion). Each sub-family states its leaf axis (section 3.4; F4.7 by object served); below the leaf sits the kind (the generic form, C11; 67 kinds in the worked tree) and then the named form (tradition form, style, iconic design or maker's design, with `maker_bound`, and a required tradition value where the name is a tradition's). At most four levels below the category; F4.2, F4.5, F4.6 and F4.7 may drop the leaf level (`leaf = null`, C5(b)). Tradition is a multi-valued C8 scene tag and a generated view, never a level, with no C2(c) exception kind for butsudan or any other tradition name (F-3, CV-27); occasion, life-cycle stage, iconography, setting, material, size and capacity (C6), installation (C12), power source (C10), user (adult, child, pet; C6), region, era, maker and price band stay off the tree. No tier sits on any kind: steel gauge is an attribute with its standard named, wood and metal are `material`, relic class is a classification attribute, and the kosher scroll grade is one row of the C16 grading-scale registry entered on one fetched source, with no grade value stored (F-19, CV-34). One cremation-urn kind carries `capacity_class`, with no keepsake or companion exception (F-18). Makers are recorded in the shared domain maker ledger, uncapped, on the CONVENTIONS_v1 C1 columns and classes (F-20); published deck designs and makers' urn and casket designs are maker-bound named forms, uncapped (C2, CV-7). Order inside a leaf follows C7 as amended by CV-21; the tradition grouping is a generated view (section 8). Boundaries follow the step 14 note of CATEGORIES_v0_7 (K-107 to K-117), K-1, K-180, B4-O2, F-17 and F4-O1 (section 9).

**Remaining risks.**
- *Relics and human remains.* Step 7 sends religious relics to F4, while the same note says "Human specimens are OUT" (K-79). A first-class relic is part of a body (S31). No decision says which sentence wins; kind 8 stays as filed, and the conflict is logged for the owner queue (section 12).

The mezuzah case follows the scroll into F4 either way; whether it should then sit in F4.7 rather than F4.3c (where shops and the choice level treat it as a compared object, section 4b) is not decided by any line (section 12).

The key words them as tests of the object's form and how it is sold; placers will need the seller's stated purpose on the fetched page.
