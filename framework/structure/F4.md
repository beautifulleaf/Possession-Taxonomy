# F4 Religious & Ceremonial Objects — structure memo (research draft)

*Stage 2 research for the owner. Research, not a decision. Prepared under framework/CATEGORIES_v0_6.md, BRIEF.md and framework/STAGE2_MEMO_TEMPLATE.md. Owner emphasis for F4: objects by function in practice (devotion, ritual, funerary), with the religious tradition as a facet; this memo argues whether tradition is a modifier view or a first axis, with evidence from museum cataloguing. The 37 pages fetched in this batch are listed in verify/out/S2_F4_sources.tsv; the short keys S01 to S37 below point to its rows in that order (key table at the end of section 1). Call count: 12 search calls (41 queries in all) and 40 fetches; the fetch cap was reached, so some planned checks (an Orthodox icon shop, a Hindu mandir retailer with a fuller tree, a Chinese and a Korean category tree) were not made. Two shops (S34 Taiwan, S35 Korea) returned no category tree and carry no claims. Evidence comes from museum thesauri and standards (Getty AAT, Nomenclature), five museums, a global product taxonomy and a trade classification, retailers in the US, Germany, the Netherlands, Japan, India and Israel, an antiques marketplace in France, collector and auction sources, a Japanese e-commerce market report, an enthusiast site (tarot) and a reference work.*

**One-line result.** Divide F4 by the role the object plays in the practice (six sub-families: sacred focus and images, personal devotion aids, rite vessels and implements, light and incense and offerings, divination tools, and the dead), then by object family as shops and museums name it (leaf), then by the generic type a person owns (kind, with tradition-bound kinds such as butsudan, mezuzah, rosary and tasbih kept as kinds because they are generic types in their own tradition), then by named form (style or iconic design). **Tradition is a modifier and a generated view, not a level**; occasion, material, user (adult, child, pet), setting (home, congregation) and price are also tags. Kinds stop where buyers compare quality or makers: the mezuzah scroll (kosher grade), the relic (class), the casket (steel gauge or wood), the cremation urn (capacity class), the altar (type). Quality tiers exist only in three narrow places and stay as modifiers; makers are incidental, with a small optional ledger for collector-grade Judaica silver and church-candle makers.

## 0. Category

**Code and name.** F4 Religious & Ceremonial Objects (domain F, Culture, Records & Meaning).

**Membership test (placement key, step 14).** "Made for **use in religious or spiritual practice, or to hold, mark or accompany the dead**: altars, shrines, icons, prayer beads, tefillin, ritual vessels, lamps, instruments and implements, incense and incense burners made for a rite, religious festival objects used in worship (menorah, diya, Advent wreath, home nativity set), tarot and oracle decks, coffins and caskets, cremation urns for people or pets, grave markers." Category line: "objects for religious practice and for the dead, worn or not, except dress and ornaments."

**Notes and rules that already touch F4.**
- Step 14 note: objects for weddings, christenings, graduations and other occasions are filed as what they are, so occasion is a modifier. Religious dress and vestments are A1. Anything worn as an ornament, including amulets worn as pendants, rings or bracelets, is A3. Decorations for any festival, religious or secular (Christmas trees and ornaments, Easter, Diwali, Halloween, Hina-matsuri displays) are B4 unless made for use in worship; a festival basket is a basket (B1). Home-fragrance incense is B4. Instruments also used outside worship, such as an organ or harmonium, are E3. Card and board games are E1. Meditation cushions and mats are B2.
- Step 7 note (F6): religious relics and rudraksha beads are F4, not specimens. Step 13 note (B10): pet cremation urns and memorials are F4. Step 4 (G2): a burial plot or niche is property; step 4 sends buildings to G2, so a mausoleum is G2 and a grave marker is F4.
- Step 15 (F3): medals, decorations and regalia of office are F3. Step 20 (F2): pictures and free-standing sculpture are F2, but step 14 is tried first. Step 21 (F1): books "including religious texts" are F1. Step 19 (F5): issued documents and keepsake papers. Step 3: groceries (communion wafers, kosher wine and matzah are food or drink, not F4).
- Appendix A modifiers that bite here: region and culture, era, price band and quality tier, brand and maker, scene and subculture, user (including pet versus person), setting, occasion (wedding, festival, season), motive (heirloom, memorabilia), material, condition, edition.

**Gaps in the key that this research hit (details in section 9).** Prayer rugs (B2 mats versus F4), singing bowls (E3 versus F4), candles made for a rite (named by neither B3 nor step 14), devotional paintings such as icons, thangkas and altarpieces (F2 versus F4, where the antiques trade and the auction trade treat them as pictures), religious medals (F3, A3 or F4), Masonic and other fraternal ritual objects (a ritual but not obviously a religion), sage and other smudge bundles (B9 or B4 or F4), and memorial jewellery holding ashes (A3 by the ornament rule).

### 1.7 Where the sources agree and where they split

- **Agree.** Every neutral standard (AAT, Nomenclature, Google, UNSPSC) classes these objects by what they are for (worship, devotion, funeral, wedding), and puts **no religious tradition above the object**. Single-tradition shops, whose whole shop is the tradition, divide by object (Judaica.com, HECK, DharmaShop, Hasegawa). Funeral shops divide by object, material and user, not religion (S20, S21, S26, S27).
- **Split.** (1) Occasion versus role: Nomenclature makes Funerary, Religious, Holiday and Wedding sibling subclasses (a purpose axis); AAT makes religious objects divide into cult, devotional, liturgical and relics (a role axis). (2) Tradition: the antiques market (S22) and curated museum departments (S09, S37) name the tradition first, but the museum that holds Judaica as a collecting area (S37) physically displays it by form. (3) Funerary: Nomenclature separates it from "formal religious worship"; Google lists it beside religion; F4 joins them. (4) Pets: funeral shops run a parallel pet tree (S27) while F4's own note files pet urns here.

## 2. The first axis people choose by

**What owners and buyers decide first.** Four decisions come in this order, but the first is usually settled before any category tree is opened.

1. **Which practice the object is for (tradition).** Buyers settle this by choosing where to shop, not by clicking a category: Judaica.com, The Catholic Shop, HECK, Muslim Mall, DharmaShop, Hasegawa and Sri Diya are each one tradition (or two neighbouring ones, as in Japan) and none has a tradition level in its own menu (S13, S19, S25, S28, S29, S16, S12). In shops that serve every practice at once, the tradition becomes a navigation level only in the antiques trade (S22) and in curated museum departments (S09).
2. **What the object does in the rite.** Inside a single-tradition shop this is the top menu: tallit, tefillin, mezuzah, kiddush cup, shofar, candlesticks (S13); chalice, monstrance, censer, eternal light (S25); butsudan, ihai, kamidana, butsugu, incense (S16, S17); singing bowls, prayer flags, malas, incense, statues (S29). In every neutral standard it is also the top split (S01, S02, S04, S05, S10, S11).
3. **Occasion, where one exists.** Occasion is a menu in its own right in three shops (Judaica.com Wedding, High Holidays, Kids and lifecycle; The Catholic Shop Baptism, Confirmation, First Holy Communion, Wedding, Christmas; HECK occasion candles) (S13, S19, S25). The framework already makes occasion a modifier (step 14 note), and the evidence supports a tag: the same Hanukkah lamp is a Hanukkah object and a candle holder.
4. **Material, size and price.** Material is the first navigational filter for urns (Memorials.com: wood 2,139, ceramic 1,769, metal 1,621, marble 1,053; House of Urns: powder-coated steel, stainless steel, brass, bronze, ceramic) and for coffins (sarg-urne.de by wood) (S20, S21, S26), a sub-menu under the object in Judaica.com (S13), a top-level axis in Sri Diya (S12) and the tasbih (S28). Size matters when parts must fit: a mezuzah scroll comes in four sizes and "it is important for the scroll to fit comfortably into the case" (S32).

**What the questions look like.** For funeral goods the question is about the person or pet and the plan for the ashes: House of Urns opens with "Select pet or person" and an Urn Finder (S21); Memorials.com lists keepsake, companion, scattering, biodegradable and urn vault as separate categories (S20); casket guides weigh steel gauge against hardwood and warn that sealing adds cost without benefit (S33). For ritual goods the question is what the rite needs and for whom: Japanese altar pages sort altars by style (modern, traditional karaki, traditional gold-leaf, compact, wall-hung) and offer price bands (S16); the e-commerce data show buyers split by purpose, with incense, candles and beads at the low band and altars carrying 56.6 percent of sales (S18).

**Conclusion for the axis.** The first axis a buyer *uses* is the tradition (resolved by venue) and then the object's role in the practice. The first axis a *neutral* catalogue can use is the role, because a neutral catalogue has to hold all practices at once. The role axis also survives where tradition does not apply: tarot and oracle decks (S24, S10), neopagan tools (S30), funeral goods (S20, S27) and secular memorial objects.

## 3. Candidate structures

### 3.0 The question: is tradition a modifier view or a first axis?

Evidence for **modifier view** (recommended):
1. **Every neutral museum and trade standard splits by function, never tradition.** AAT places ceremonial objects under object genres by function, with cult, devotional, liturgical and relic children (S01, S02); Nomenclature uses Religious, Funerary, Wedding, Holiday subclasses (S03, S04, S05); Google has Religious Items, Memorial Ceremony Supplies and Wedding Ceremony Supplies (S10); UNSPSC lists commodities by object (S11). Tradition appears in none of those trees as a level; it appears only inside term names (rosary, seder plate, vajra, tankas).
2. **Museums that hold a tradition as a collecting area still catalogue the object by type.** The Rubin lists "ritual objects" as a classification inside a Himalayan museum (S07); the Israel Museum has a Jewish Art department and the subject "Hanukkah lamp" (S09); the MFA defines Judaica by tradition yet hangs a Hanukkah lamp among European decorative arts and puts the shofar in the Musical Instruments Gallery (S37). Nomenclature itself (S06) requires cross-indexing because one object has several legitimate parents.
3. **One function recurs across traditions.** Prayer beads are the rosary (S19), tasbih (S28), mala (S29) and juzu (S16); the ritual lamp is the menorah (S01, S09), the eternal light (S25), the butter-lamp holder (S34) and the lamp in a Hindu pooja shop (S12); the household shrine is the butsudan, the kamidana (S16, S17) and the home temple (S15); the incense burner is the censer (S25), the koro (S16, S17) and the UNSPSC commodity (S11); the ritual bell is the altar bell (S04), the ghanta (S01), the singing bowl (S29) and the rin (S16). A tradition-first tree would repeat each of these leaves five or six times.
4. **Objects belong to more than one tradition, and to practices that are not religions.** A Buddhist shop sells rudraksha malas (S29); a Japanese shop holds Buddhist and Shinto goods side by side (S16, S17); the Proantic split has to add Masonic Art and Other Religions & Rituals (S22); tarot and neopagan tools have no church (S24, S30); funeral goods are not tied to a faith (S20, S26).
5. **A tradition-first tree is lopsided.** In the one source that does it, Christian Art is 5,054 of 5,811 objects and Judaica 58 (S22). A top-level split with that skew gives owners of Islamic or Buddhist goods almost no structure and cannot hold the long tail.
6. **The framework already rules it out.** Appendix A lists "region and culture" and "scene and subculture" as modifiers, and rule S4 forbids modifiers splitting the top level. The same logic applies to inner levels (S2 memo shared rules).

Evidence for a **first axis** (and why it does not win):
1. Dealers, collectors and single-tradition museums navigate by tradition (S22, S23, S09). *Answer:* that is a **view**, and the framework allows generated views. One tag per kind and named form gives the Proantic user "Judaica", "Islamic art" and "Other religions" for free.
2. Some objects are only meaningful inside one tradition and have no generic English name (butsudan, kamidana, mezuzah, tefillin, tankas, ihai). *Answer:* those are **tradition-bound kinds**: the kind is named in the tradition's own term, sits in a leaf with its functional cousins, and carries a required tradition tag. Tradition is then derived, not a level.
3. Within a tradition, sects change the object: a Sufi-order tasbih (Tijani, Jerrahi, Naqshbandi) (S28); the Chabad script of a mezuzah scroll (S32); a Shinto soreisha altar rather than a Buddhist butsudan (S16). *Answer:* those are **named forms** with a tradition (or order) tag.

**Recommendation on the question:** tradition is a **multi-valued tag and a generated view** attached to kinds and named forms (several values allowed: rudraksha malas carry Hindu and Buddhist), with a required value on tradition-bound kinds. It is never a level. The same holds for occasion (Hanukkah, Diwali, Advent, Obon), life-cycle stage (birth, wedding, death) and setting (home, congregation, institution).

### 3.1 Candidate A — role in the practice first (recommended)

- **Levels and axes.** Sub-family: *the role the object plays for the practitioner* (six values). Leaf: *object family as museums and shops name it*. Kind: *generic type a person can own*, including tradition-bound kinds. Named form: *style or iconic design*.
- **Sub-families.** F4.1 Altars, shrines and sacred images (what is set up to be venerated). F4.2 Personal devotion aids (what the practitioner holds or carries to pray). F4.3 Ritual vessels, implements and instruments (what is handled in the rite). F4.4 Light, incense and offerings (what is lit, burned or set out). F4.5 Divination and esoteric tools (what is read or cast). F4.6 The dead (what holds, marks or accompanies the dead).
- **Precedence inside F4.** Test in this order: the dead, divination, offerings, sacred focus, rite implement, personal aid. So a memorial lamp that is lit in a rite stays a ritual lamp (F4.4) with a memorial tag, while an urn that holds ashes is F4.6 whatever its decoration.
- **Examples (14 real kinds).** butsudan (S16), kamidana (S17), crucifix (S19), thangka (S29), rosary (S19), tasbih (S28), prayer rug (S28), chalice (S25), kiddush cup (S13), shofar (S13), Hanukkah lamp (S09), censer (S25), tarot deck (S10), cremation urn (S10).
- **Where it breaks.** (1) A Hanukkah lamp is both a ritual lamp (F4.4) and a festival object; handled by the occasion tag. (2) Tefillin and a mezuzah are worn or fixed objects with a text inside; handled as F4.3 scroll-and-law objects with the text-bearing part a separate kind. (3) A sanctuary lamp and an altar candle could be F4.1 (sacred furniture) or F4.4; rule: lit objects are F4.4. (4) A pet urn: same F4.6 kind with a user tag, as step 13's note directs.

### 3.2 Candidate B — occasion or purpose first (Nomenclature style)

- **Levels and axes.** Subclass: *the occasion or purpose the object serves* (religious worship, funerary, wedding, holiday, party, organizational) (S03). Then object term (S04, S05).
- **Examples (12).** altar, altar bell, prayer wheel, religious lamp (S04); burial shroud, coffin plate, funeral pall, tombstone (S05); breaking glass, ring pillow, marriage bowl (S01, S10); Advent wreath, menorah (framework step 14).
- **Where it breaks.** (1) A Hanukkah lamp is religious and holiday at once. (2) A Yahrzeit candle is a ritual lamp used in mourning. (3) A christening plate or a first-communion ribbon are occasion objects, but step 14's note says occasion is a modifier, so the framework cannot file by it. (4) The Nomenclature guide admits the problem and works around it by cross-indexing (S06); this taxonomy has one home per object, so it cannot.

### 3.3 Candidate C — tradition first (Proantic style)

- **Levels and axes.** Level 1: *the religion or practice* (Christian, Islamic, Jewish, Buddhist, Hindu, Shinto, neopagan, Masonic, none). Level 2: object family. Level 3: kind (S22 offers only the first level).
- **Examples (12).** Judaism: mezuzah scroll (S32), kiddush cup (S13), Hanukkah lamp (S09); Christianity: crucifix (S19), monstrance (S25), rosary (S19); Islam: tasbih, prayer rug (S28); Buddhism: butsudan, juzu (S16), mala (S29); neopagan: athame (S30).
- **Where it breaks.** (1) Prayer beads and incense burners recur in every branch and would be stored five times; the one-home rule needs a single parent. (2) Rudraksha malas in a Buddhist shop belong to two traditions (S29). (3) Tarot decks have no tradition (S10, S24); secular memorial goods have none either (S20). (4) The skew in S22 (87 percent Christian) leaves most branches almost empty.

## 4. Depth and size

These are estimates from the shape of the sources; nothing was counted item by item.

- **Generic kinds.** Roughly **220 to 320**. Anchors: the AAT religious and ceremonial branches list about 250 object genres (S01, S02), of which texts (F1), ceremonial costume (A1), ceremonial weapons (E4 or F3) and a few buildings fall outside F4; Nomenclature's Funerary subclass shows about 15 terms and its Religious subclass at least 40 (the fetched page truncated the list) (S04, S05); the Japanese altar-fittings tree has about 15 butsugu kinds plus about 10 kamidana fittings (S16, S17); Hindu pooja, Catholic church supply and Judaica shops each list 20 to 40 distinct object types (S12, S25, S13). Tradition-bound kinds are probably 30 to 40 percent of the total.
- **Named forms.** Roughly **600 to 1,500**: altar styles (six in S16), prayer rug types (six in S02), Hanukkah lamp styles, rosary and mala types by bead and count, mezuzah and tefillin script styles (S32), urn lines, and tarot and oracle anchors. Individual decks are not named forms: Aeclectic lists about 2,000 decks (S24), which are products; only iconic designs (Rider-Waite, Thoth, Marseille) are forms.
- **Where depth concentrates.** (1) Rite vessels and implements, because every tradition has a set (Catholic liturgical set S25; Judaica S13; Buddhist and Shinto butsugu and shingu S16, S17). (2) Light and incense. (3) The dead, where funeral shops carry hundreds of urn designs but few kinds (S20, S26, S27).
- **Breadth by leaf.** Broad (30 or more kinds): ritual vessels and implements; ritual lamps, candles and incense; sacred statues and images. Medium (10 to 30): household altars and shrines; prayer aids; sacred sound; funerary furnishings and markers. Narrow (under 10): relics and reliquaries; prayer beads (four tradition-bound kinds); urns (five or six); coffins; card decks; other divination tools.
- **Depth.** Four levels below the category at most (sub-family, leaf, kind, named form). A leaf is also a kind when the family name is already a generic type (holy water font, tombstone); in that case the leaf level is omitted and the sub-family has three levels for it.

## 4b. Choice level

**A kind stops at the level where buyers actually compare quality or makers.** Evidence, sub-family by sub-family:

- **Mezuzah and tefillin (F4.3).** The scroll and the case are compared separately: cases by style and material (Judaica.com sub-menus by glass, metal, wood, ceramic, S13), scrolls by size class and **kosher grade** (aJudaica: "all aJudaica mezuzah parchments are 100% kosher. The terms: basic, upgraded and mehudar refer to graded levels of excellence", S32). So *mezuzah case* and *mezuzah scroll* are two kinds; script style (Ashkenazi, Sephardic, Ari, Chabad) is a named form or tag; the grade is a tier attribute (section 5).
- **Cremation urns (F4.6).** The funeral shops split by capacity or purpose first and material second: keepsake, companion, scattering, biodegradable, urn vault (S20), with material as the filter (S20, S21, S27). Kinds: cremation urn (full size), keepsake urn, companion urn, scattering urn, urn vault. Material (wood, marble, bronze, ceramic, glass), theme (sport, hunting, butterfly) and user (adult, child, pet) are attributes. Pet urns are the same kinds with a user tag, since the funeral trade runs a parallel tree (S27) and the framework note files pet urns here.
- **Coffins and caskets (F4.6).** Buyers compare by material and by measurable spec: steel gauge, stainless, copper, pine, oak, mahogany, biodegradable, rental (S33). One kind (*coffin and casket*) with these as attributes; hardwood and gauge are tier attributes, not kinds.
- **Altars (F4.1).** Japanese buyers compare altar type (modern, karaki, gold-leaf, compact, wall-hung, soreisha) (S16). Kinds: household altar, butsudan, kamidana; the types are named forms.
- **Prayer beads (F4.2).** Shops make the tradition-bound kind the unit (rosary S19, tasbih S28, mala S29, juzu S16) and then compare by material (wood, stone, bone, sterling silver, seed and nut S28; bodhi seed, rudraksha, sandalwood S29). So the four types are kinds and the materials are attributes; rudraksha and bodhi seed are named forms because the material is the form (step 7 note).
- **Statues and images (F4.1).** Retailers split by the figure shown (Mary, Christ, saints S19; deities S15). The *figure* is a tag (iconography); the kinds are statue, icon, thangka, crucifix, scroll, because makers and material tiers are compared at that level.
- **Candles and incense (F4.4).** HECK lists altar candles by maker and wax content (S25: "100 percent Ceresin" or "10 percent beeswax"); occasion candles (baptism, communion, Easter) are one kind (*ritual candle*) with an occasion tag. Incense is a kind (*incense*) with a form attribute (stick, cone, resin, church grade).
- **Divination (F4.5).** Aeclectic compares decks by theme, publisher and style (S24); the kind stops at *tarot deck*, *oracle deck*, *Lenormand deck*.

**The choice level differs between sub-families.** It is deepest in F4.3 (every vessel and implement is a separate kind because shops list them separately and tradition sets define them) and shallowest in F4.5 and in urns (a few kinds, many named forms or attributes).

## 5. Quality and tier

**Recommendation: preference for the category as a whole**, with three narrow ladders attached as modifiers on specific kinds. There is no category-wide recognised ordering.

| Where | Ladder | Type | Source and how it appears |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mezuzah scroll, tefillin, other handwritten scroll goods | basic, upgraded, mehudar (vendors also use "superior") | consensus-ranked, within one tradition, set by workmanship of the script; all grades are valid | S32; a modifier on the scroll kind named "kosher grade". |
| Relics | first class (body, Passion relics), second class (belonged to or used by the saint), third class (touched to a first or second class relic) | consensus-ranked (church custom), by provenance, not quality | S31; a modifier named "relic class" on *relic*. |
| Coffins and caskets | 20-gauge versus 18-gauge steel, stainless, copper and bronze; pine/poplar, oak/cherry, mahogany/walnut | spec-ranked (gauge) and material ladder; guides stress that the practical difference between 18 and 20 gauge is small | S33; a modifier named "gauge or wood" on the *coffin and casket* kind. |

Everywhere else, price band is the only ordering in the sources (Nint's three bands, S18; Sri Diya and Muslim Mall list no grades, S12, S28). The tier never becomes a level: a *mehudar mezuzah scroll* is a mezuzah scroll kind with a grade modifier. A generated view may list the three ladders together.

## 6. Makers and brands

**Recommendation: incidental**, with two small exceptions.

- **Where makers appear.** HECK lists altar candles by manufacturer (Kopschitz, Wiedemann, Jaspers) (S25); The Catholic Shop lists lines such as Swarovski and St. Joseph's Studio (S19); Hasegawa offers a choice of four of its own altar brands (S16); Funeral Products B.V. lists design houses and artists (Atlantis Memorials, Sieben Design, Geert Kunen) (S27); Aeclectic lets users find decks by publisher (Lo Scarabeo, Llewellyn, US Games) (S24); the Israeli auction names the designers of Hanukkah lamps (Ludwig Wolpert, Arie Ofir, Shaul Nameri) (S36).
- **Identity versus incidental.** In every shop the buyer first chooses the object and its tradition, then the style; the maker comes last. The only place where the maker is part of what is sought is the **collector and auction end of Judaica silver and design** (S36) and the **tarot publisher** (S24).
- **Maker ledger.** Not needed at start. A small ledger would serve two groups: Judaica silversmiths and designers, and tarot and oracle publishers. If the owner wants collector-grade depth, rows would be maker, known-for kinds, scene or tradition tags, tier, country and era, each attested. Church-candle and urn makers can be a plain column on named forms.
- **Platform dependence.** There is none, with one exception: scroll and case sizes must fit (S32), a compatibility spec that belongs on the mezuzah scroll and case kinds as a size class.

## 8. Order inside a leaf

**Recommendation: canonical (generic) kind first, then tradition-bound kinds grouped by tradition in a fixed alphabetical order, then named forms by chronology or regional style.**

Reason: AAT and Nomenclature order terms alphabetically inside a parent (S01, S02, S04, S05), a rule that avoids ranking faiths; museums order objects by form and period (the MFA hangs Hanukkah lamps in the 18th-century European gallery, S37); the AAT's own named forms for prayer rugs are typed, not ranked (S02). Retailers order by frequency or by the use-sequence of the rite (Judaica.com runs from Shabbat through the High Holidays, S13), which suits a shop but not a catalogue. Within a leaf the order would be: generic kind (household altar, prayer beads, incense burner), then Buddhist, Christian, Hindu, Islamic, Jewish, Shinto and so on in alphabetical order, then the named forms of each kind by date.

## 9. Boundaries

The closest cross-boundary objects, with precedence sentences. Items marked **owner question** are not decided by the current key.

1. **F4 versus A1 Clothing (religious dress).** Kippah, tallit, tzitzit, kufi, vestments, habits and baptism or communion dresses are dress and go to A1; tefillin are named in step 14 and go to F4 although they are strapped to the body. *Precedence: a garment worn as dress is A1, even when only worn in worship; a strapped box, scroll or wrapped ritual object that step 14 names is F4.*
2. **F4 versus A3 Jewellery.** Cross, hamsa, Star of David and mezuzah pendants, rosary bracelets, tasbih bracelets (S28) and ash pendants (S21, S27) are worn ornaments and go to A3. *Precedence: anything worn as an ornament is A3, and a prayer string kept in the hand is F4; a rosary is F4 and a rosary bracelet is A3.* **Owner question:** ash jewellery is worn but holds the dead; the ornament rule sends it to A3.
3. **F4 versus B4 Decor.** Christmas trees, ornaments, Diwali and Hina-matsuri displays, home-fragrance incense and garden statues are B4. *Precedence: incense or a lamp made for a rite is F4, home-fragrance incense and decorative candles are B4; a statue of a figure made to be venerated is F4, a garden statue is B4.* Bon lanterns (S16, S17) and a nativity set are F4 by step 14's list ("used in worship").
4. **F4 versus B2 Soft Furnishings (mats and cushions).** Step 14's note sends meditation cushions and mats to B2 but does not name prayer rugs. *Precedence proposed: a rug made as a prayer rug (kibla niche, travel, children's) is F4; a meditation cushion is B2.* **Owner question.**
5. **F4 versus E3 Musical Instruments (sound makers).** Step 14's note keeps organs and harmoniums in E3. Shofar, altar bells, mokugyo, rin and ghanta are made for rites and go to F4; the singing bowl is sold for meditation and sound baths (S29). *Precedence: an instrument made for a rite is F4, an instrument also used outside worship is E3.* **Owner question:** singing bowls.
6. **F4 versus F1 and F5.** A published religious text is F1 (step 21); the text inside a mezuzah or tefillin is handwritten and is part of the ritual object (F4). A ketubah or baptism certificate is a document or art and not F4; a prayer card is a printed devotional card (S10) and goes to F4 under step 14 as a devotional object. *Precedence: a bound or printed religious text is F1; a scroll or card made to be fixed, worn or used in a rite is F4.* **Owner question:** prayer cards and holy cards.
7. **F4 versus F2 Art.** Icons, thangkas, altarpieces and crucifixes are pictures or sculpture but are made for devotion. The antiques trade lists icons (284) and religious paintings (3,035) under Painting (S22) while AAT lists altarpieces and tankas as religious visual works (S02). *Precedence: step 14 is tried before step 20, so a picture or statue made to be venerated is F4 and the same image kept as decoration is F2; the owner's motive does not decide, the maker's purpose does.* **Owner question:** whether an old-master religious painting and an auction-house altarpiece are F2 or F4, since the framework key (S5) tests the object, not the motive.
8. **F4 versus F3 Medals.** A devotional medal on a chain is A3; a loose commemorative or religious medal is F3. *Precedence: worn is A3; a medal struck as a collectible or award is F3; a devotional medal is a kind only if worn as an ornament.* **Owner question.**
9. **F4 versus F6 and G2.** Relics and rudraksha beads are F4 (step 7 note); a mausoleum or burial plot is G2; a gravestone is F4. *Precedence: a natural object venerated as a relic is F4; the plot and the building are G2.*
10. **F4 versus B10 and B1/B3.** Pet urns and pet memorials are F4 (B10 note); a candle made for a rite is F4 as a lamp but step 14 does not name candles, and B3 does not name them either. *Precedence proposed: a candle made for a rite or devotion is F4.* **Owner question:** votive and altar candles.
11. **F4 versus J (groceries) and B9.** Communion wafers, kosher wine and matzah are groceries (J) and not F4; sage and palo santo smudge bundles are B9 or B4 unless made for a rite. *Precedence: food and drink as eaten are out; a ritual implement that holds them (chalice, seder plate) is F4.*
12. **Other cross-boundary notes.** Masonic and other fraternal ritual objects (S22) are not a religion; step 14 says "religious or spiritual practice", and regalia of office are F3. **Owner question.** Wedding items (S10: aisle runners, flower girl baskets, ring pillows) are weddings, not rites in the sense of step 14, and stay with what they are (B4, B1, B2); a breaking glass or a ketubah used in a Jewish rite is F4 or F5 by form.

## 10. Recommendation and open questions

**Recommended structure.** Candidate A: six sub-families by role in the practice (F4.1 Altars, shrines and sacred images; F4.2 Personal devotion aids; F4.3 Ritual vessels, implements and instruments; F4.4 Light, incense and offerings; F4.5 Divination and esoteric tools; F4.6 The dead), then leaf (object family), then kind (a generic type, including tradition-bound kinds named in the tradition's own term), then named form (style or iconic design). **Maximum depth four levels below the category**, with many leaves stopping at the kind.

Off the tree and carried as modifiers or views: tradition and branch (multi-valued tag, generated view), occasion and festival, life-cycle stage, setting, material, user (adult, child, pet), region, era, maker, price and quality tier. Tier ladders (kosher scroll grade, relic class, casket gauge and wood) as modifiers on the kinds named in section 5. Maker ledger optional and small. Choice level: scroll and case separate; cremation urn split by capacity and purpose (full, keepsake, companion, scattering, vault); coffin one kind; prayer beads four tradition-bound kinds; statue, icon, thangka and crucifix as kinds; ritual candle as one kind with occasion tags.

**Owner decisions needed.**
1. Confirm that **tradition is a tag and view, not a level**, and that tradition-bound kinds (butsudan, kamidana, mezuzah, rosary, tasbih, mala, juzu, ihai) are kept as kinds with a required tradition tag.
2. Rule on prayer rugs (B2 or F4), singing bowls (E3 or F4), prayer cards and holy cards (F4, F1 or F5), and candles made for a rite (F4 or B3).
3. Rule on devotional images: are icons, thangkas, altarpieces and religious paintings F4 (maker's purpose) or F2 (the antiques trade's view)?
4. Rule on religious medals (F3, A3 or F4) and ash jewellery (A3 or F4).
5. Rule on Masonic and other fraternal ritual objects, and on smudge bundles and similar herbal ritual goods.
6. Confirm that the dead are one sub-family covering coffins, urns, markers and memorial furnishings, with pets a user tag.
7. Decide whether the three tier ladders (kosher grade, relic class, casket gauge and wood) should be stored at all; they are narrow and sourced from vendors in this batch (S31, S32, S33), so an independent standard would be needed to attest a ladder.
8. Decide whether a small maker ledger for Judaica silver and tarot publishers is wanted.

**Not done in this batch (cap reached).** An Orthodox icon and church-goods retailer, a fuller Hindu retailer tree (the two Indian shops fetched gave menus or banners only), a Chinese and a Korean category tree (S34, S35 returned no trees), a Sikh, Taoist or Zoroastrian source, and a second collector marketplace. These would test whether the six sub-families hold for traditions with more domestic and less congregational practice.
