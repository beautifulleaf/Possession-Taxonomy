## 0. Category

**Code and name:** J1 Meat & Poultry.

**Membership test** (CATEGORIES_v0_6, step 3, first branch): *meat, poultry and game, fresh, frozen, cured, smoked or cooked, including whole cuts, mince, offal, sausages, charcuterie, hams and jerky.* Category line: "meat, poultry and game as bought, fresh, frozen, cured, smoked or cooked, including charcuterie and hams, divided by species, cut and grade (for example USDA and JMGA grades, breed, feeding and ageing)."

**Rules and notes that touch J1:**
- Step 1 (OUT) note: "curing, smoking, ageing, fermenting, drying, salting, pickling, tinning, freezing or plain cooking of one main ingredient does not make a food prepared: … hams raw or cooked, sausages and charcuterie … are groceries". A prepared food is "made by cooking or combining ingredients into something eaten or drunk as it is" (dishes, ready meals, "dumplings and other prepared dishes") and is left to Culinary.
- Step 3 note: each grocery is "filed once, as the product as bought, divided by product and by the quality of the ingredient, and Culinary links to it". "Sauces, condiments, stocks, pastes and cooking fats are J5 whatever they are made from (… stock cubes, lard, dripping)". "Dried or cured meat and fish are groceries even when sold salted, roasted or as snacks." "Pasta and cooking sauces in jars, with or without meat, are sauces (J5)." "A set, gift box or hamper is filed by its item of greatest value, and is OUT if that item is a prepared food." Food made for animals is B10, including "frozen feeder animals" and pet chews.
- Step 3, J2: shellfish and crustaceans sold alive, roe and caviar are J2. J3: eggs are J3. J5: "tofu, tempeh, seitan and other plant proteins and meat alternatives".
- Step 2 note: sports-nutrition and meal-replacement products in every form (including protein bars) are H2.
- Step 1 and the B10 memo: a living animal is OUT; live feeders are OUT.
- Framework rule S2 (generic types): models, cuts and designs are named forms under a kind (section 4b and open question 2 say how this memo reads "cuts" here). Rule S4 and Appendix A: region, era, price and tier, brand and maker, scene, condition, material and occasion are modifiers, never levels.
- Step 3 note and step 5 (spreads and pastes): "sauces, condiments, stocks, pastes and cooking fats are J5 whatever they are made from", and the J5 branch lists "sweet and savoury spreads, sauces, condiments, stocks and pastes from any source". This governs spreadable meat pastes (section 9, P8). The step 1 note makes "tinning" and "plain cooking of one main ingredient" grocery processes, so plain-cooked and tinned meat is J1 (section 3, J1-D).
- Proposed domain-J conventions JC1 to JC7 (`_conventions.md`; owner approval pending) and the J section of `_framework_conflicts.md`: used in this memo as working rules and marked `OWNER:` where they are not corrections.
- BRIEF.md (owner, 2026-10-17): groceries are "judged by the quality of the ingredient (for example high-quality meat: breed, cut, grade, origin, age)"; "Grocery products that the Culinary taxonomy also holds (… cured meats …) are described here as bought, with their quality grade, and link to the Culinary kind."

### 1.5 Where the sources agree and where they split

**Agree.** (1) For raw meat the animal comes first, and then the cut: HS [S18], IMPS [S37], Interbev [S7], AHDB [S8], wholesalers [S14], Spanish shops [S21]. (2) Every national cut system rests on the same primal logic (US eight primals [S25]; Japanese 9, 13 or 15 parts [S5][S16]; French and British charts [S9][S32]) but the names and cutting lines differ. (3) Official grades are measured on the **carcass**, not on a retail cut: USDA's standard is titled and written for carcasses [S2]; Kobe's bylaws say "beef carcass grading is determined by yield and meat quality scores" [S31]; a Hanwoo restaurant says "one grade is stamped on the whole carcass" [S22]. (4) Cured meats and sausages are defined by process, not by species: HS 0210 [S18], IMPS 500/600/800 [S37], Leitsätze [S17], Japanese labelling [S15].

**Split.** (1) Species-first all the way down (Ocado puts Parma ham and salami inside pork [S35]) against process-first for made products (Japanese wholesaler "加工肉" [S14]; Spanish Jamonería and Charcutería [S21]; French Delicatessen [S28]; IMPS 500–800 [S37]). (2) Offal is its own group in HS 0206, IMPS 700, Interbev's "produits tripiers" poster and the Chinese by-products part [S18][S37][S7][S11], but a sub-category inside pork at Ocado [S35]. (3) D'Artagnan's single Type facet mixes species, breed, feeding, grade and product class [S12]; Crowd Cow splits beef by feeding first [S13]. (4) Sausages are grouped by process in Germany [S17], by composition and pork piece in Japan [S15], and by name and origin at retail [S1][S14][S28]. (5) The same name can mean different cuts: US and UK "brisket" differ [S32]; the Taiwanese guide puts 牛腩 under flank [S30], while another Taiwanese shop page (seen in a search excerpt only, not fetched) equates 牛腩 with brisket; the guide calls 翼板 flat iron [S30], and the same excerpt calls it chuck flap. (6) Ready-to-cook and ready-to-eat lines sit in the meat tree at retail [S1][S35][S36], but the framework sends them to Culinary (section 9).

## 2. The first axis people choose by

**Raw meat: the animal first, then the cut or use, then quality.**
- Navigation order: Ocado opens with species (Beef, Chicken, Pork, Lamb) and then use classes (steaks, mince, roasting joints, stewing) [S1][S35][S36]; Spanish shops open with ternera, cerdo, pollo, cordero [S21]; the Japanese wholesaler opens with 牛肉, 豚肉, 鶏肉, その他 [S14]; HS and IMPS open with species [S18][S37]; Interbev and AHDB publish per species [S7][S8].
- Filter order on specialist stores: D'Artagnan lists species/breed/grade ("Type") first, then Cut, then Bone/Skin, Fresh/Frozen, Cooking Method, Portion, Brand [S12]; Crowd Cow splits beef by **feeding** first (pasture-raised, grass-fed) and then by butcher's cut and cooking [S13]. These are quality-first stores, and both still use the animal as the first break.
- Buyer questions in guides: "cut, grade, marbling and packaging" [S23]; a Japanese guide teaches the position on the carcass first (four big blocks, then the fine cuts) [S16]; a Korean restaurant says cut, ageing and thickness outweigh the grade [S22]; a Chinese-language guide describes each cut by cooking function (grilling, braising, stewing, hotpot) [S30].

**Cured meats and sausages: the product type first, then the origin or sale name.** A buyer of ham asks first for the type (Ibérico, serrano, Parma, cooked) and then the seal colour [S4][S21]; German and Japanese rules define sausages by process class [S17][S15]; French retail separates saucissons, hams, dried meat, cured meat, rillettes and terrines [S28].

**Order of decisions I infer (estimate):** animal or product type → cut or use → quality scheme (grade, breed, feeding, ageing, sale name) → presentation (bone, skin, fresh or frozen, portion) → origin and maker. Quality is not a first split in the large mainstream tree [S1] but is the first split for premium Iberian ham (the seal colour is a legal feeding × breed class [S4]).

## 3. Candidate structures

All three use the fixed level names (category, sub-family, leaf, kind, named form) and at most four levels below J1. Quality grade, breed, feeding, ageing, sale name, farming type, state (fresh or frozen), bone and skin, region and appellation are never levels (framework rule S4, Appendix A, C4(b)); they are attributes with a named standard, tags or ledger entries (sections 5 and 6).

### Candidate A — species first

- Level 1 sub-family (axis: **the animal**): Beef & veal; Pork; Lamb, mutton & goat; Poultry; Game; Other meats.
- Level 2 leaf (axis: **product form**): fresh or frozen cuts; offal; mince; cured; sausages and charcuterie; cooked.
- Kinds: cuts, cured items, sausages; named forms below.
- Example (about 12 kinds): Beef > Fresh cuts > ribeye steak, striploin steak, tenderloin, brisket, short rib, shin; Beef > Mince > ground beef; Beef > Offal > tongue; Pork > Fresh cuts > belly, collar, loin chop; Pork > Cured > dry-cured ham, bacon; Pork > Sausages > fresh pork sausage.
- Evidence for: the large UK supermarket tree [S1][S35][S36]; HS Chapter 02 by species [S18]; the category line in CATEGORIES_v0_6 names "species, cut and grade".
- **Where it breaks:** (1) many sausage and cured products mix animals — the Japanese press-ham and sausage definitions allow pork, beef, poultry, horse, sheep, goat and rabbit together [S15] — so they need a sixth, non-species sub-family, and then level 1 has two axes; (2) a charcuterie board has no single animal; (3) duck rillettes and pork rillettes would sit in different sub-families although buyers compare them as one product [S28]; (4) Ibérico ham is bought by seal colour, not as "pork" [S4]; (5) the sources that treat cured and made products as a separate group [S14][S17][S21][S28][S37] are not honoured.

### Candidate B — state of preparation first, animal at the leaf for raw meat (recommended)

- Level 1 sub-family (axis: **how the meat is prepared for sale**): J1-A Fresh & frozen meat (raw muscle, bone, offal, mince, shaped raw mince); J1-B Cured, smoked & dried whole-muscle meats; J1-C Sausages; J1-D Cooked, tinned & potted meats (plain-cooked meat; tinned meat; meat set in fat or jelly in a pot or jar; pâtés and terrines sold as a loaf or slice).
- Level 2 leaf (axis stated per sub-family): J1-A **the animal** (14 leaves, including Goat); J1-B **cure class** (raw-cured; cooked-cured; dried and jerked; cured fat; pickled and fermented †); J1-C **process class** (fresh, to be cooked; dry-cured and fermented; scalded; cooked, liver and blood). J1-D has about 12 kinds at most and skips the leaf level (C5(b)); its kinds are ordered plain-cooked, then tinned and potted, then pâtés and terrines. If it passes about 12 kinds it takes those three groups as leaves (axis: how the cooked meat is kept or sold).
- Level 3 kind: the **named retail cut** (J1-A, under the kind rule below), the **type of cured piece** (J1-B), the **sausage type** (J1-C), the **product type** (J1-D). Level 4 named form: national or sub-cut names, protected names and traditional varieties.
- **Level-1 tie-break (first yes; C5(c)).** Try in this order. (1) Comminuted meat sold as a sausage (in a casing, or formed as a sausage), whether fresh, cured, scalded or cooked: J1-C. (2) Meat that is cooked, tinned, potted or made into a pâté or terrine, as a product that exists in that form (corned beef in a tin, luncheon meat, confit or brawn set in fat or jelly, sliced roast meat, a rotisserie chicken, a pâté loaf; a spreadable potted meat or rillettes is J5, P8): J1-D. (3) A whole muscle or piece that is cured, smoked, dried or pickled, raw or cured then cooked (dry-cured ham, bacon, cooked ham, pastrami): J1-B. (4) Everything else raw, chilled or frozen: J1-A. A tin, jar or pouch is a pack attribute when the same product is also sold unpacked (a cooked ham or a sausage in a tin keeps its home, JC4(c)); it makes a J1-D kind only when the product exists as tinned-cooked-in-the-pack or as a solid potted piece set in fat or jelly (JC4(b)). A tinned ready meal is prepared (step 1). **OWNER:** the exact J1-D placement of tinned and plain-cooked meat is review question 18 (section 10).
- **Level-3 kind rule for J1-A (C5(c), review J1-3).** A kind is the animal and its retail cut or joint. A species with fewer than three cuts attested by a fetched page gets one kind, "<species>, whole or jointed", with the cut as the attribute `cut` until sources justify a split. A kind sold whole (suckling pig, suckling lamb) stays a kind of its own. Primals and sides (open question 11) follow the retail cuts in the animal's leaf. Today beef (12 attested cuts), pork (8) and chicken (whole bird, breast and leg) split by cut; lamb, goat, turkey, venison, rabbit, hare, wild boar, horse, bison and each game bird do not.
- Example kinds (about 15): J1-A Beef: ribeye steak, striploin steak, tenderloin, brisket, short rib, ground beef, tongue; J1-A Pork: belly, collar, loin chop, trotter; J1-A Duck & goose: duck breast, fatty liver; J1-A Wild boar: wild boar, whole or jointed; J1-B: dry-cured ham leg, cured pork shoulder, bacon, cooked ham; J1-C: dry-cured salami, scalded sausage; J1-D: pâté, terrine, corned beef in a tin †.
- Evidence for: HS separates raw (0201–0209) from salted, dried or smoked (0210) [S18]; IMPS puts fresh in series 100–400 and cured, dried, smoked, variety and sausage in 500–800 [S37]; the German Leitsätze group sausages and cured meats by process [S17]; Japan splits "Processed" from species in the wholesaler's list [S14] and defines ham, bacon and sausage by process [S15]; Spanish and French shops split Jamonería, Charcutería and Delicatessen from raw meat [S21][S28]. Species is kept as a required attribute (`animal`) on every kind in J1-B, J1-C and J1-D and gives a generated "by animal" view that restores the species-first path of [S1].
- **Where it breaks:** (1) offal appears in several leaves (beef tongue under Beef; pork trotter under Pork), although HS, IMPS, Interbev and the Chinese standards treat offal as its own group [S18][S37][S7][S11] — handled with a generated "offal and by-products" view and open question 9 (C14: no category-level multi-use sub-family is used); (2) a cured-and-cooked item such as pastrami or corned beef brisket sits on the border of J1-B and J1-D: the tie-break above files it in J1-B Cooked-cured when sold as a piece, and the same meat sealed in a tin is the J1-D kind "corned beef in a tin" (open question 6); (3) a minced-and-shaped raw product (burger, meatball, kebab) is raw in form but is treated as prepared by some rules [S17]: filed as the J1-A kind "shaped raw mince" if plain or only seasoned (**OWNER:** review question 7; open question 5); (4) fresh sausage has the same ingredients as mince: the test is the sausage form (tie-break step 1), so a loose or patted mince is J1-A and a sausage in a casing or formed as a sausage is J1-C.

### Candidate C — use or cooking method first

- Level 1 (axis: **how it is cooked**): Steaks and grilling; Roasting joints; Stewing and braising; Mince; Cured and cold cuts.
- Evidence for: Ocado's beef sub-categories [S36]; Interbev groups cuts by culinary destination and cooking type [S7]; D'Artagnan's Cooking Method facet [S12]; AHDB's per-cut cooking pages [S8].
- **Where it breaks:** (1) one cut has different uses in different countries: "bavette d'aloyau" is slow-cooked in the UK and flash-fried in France; chuck is a casserole cut in the UK and a grilled cut in France [S9]; (2) the Japanese guide rates the same cut for steak, barbecue, roast, sukiyaki and shabu-shabu at once [S5]; (3) cooking method, occasion and scene are modifiers (Appendix A), so they cannot be levels; (4) the same muscle (brisket) is a smoked barbecue cut, a braised joint and a corned meat. Rejected as a structure; kept as a generated view by cooking method and by cuisine (section 7).

**Modelling (C4, C16, JC1).** (a) A measured or legal scale (USDA, JMGA, BMS, Hanwoo, GB/T, RD 4/2014, Reg. 543/2008) is an **attribute with its standard named** (C4(b)). Working schema: a `standard` table with `standard_id`, name, authority, jurisdiction, `subject` (carcass, product), ordered `scale`, factors, `kinds_served`, `source_url` and date; it replaces this memo's first-draft `grade_scheme` ledger. The data records which standards a kind can carry (`kinds_served`); it stores **no grade value for a particular lot or product** (C16, which bars condition grades and certificates of an individual object). The first draft's `grade_claimed_by` field is dropped for that reason. **OWNER:** JC1(a) proposes `grade_claimed_by` on a retail product; the owner decides whether JC1 adopts it or follows C16 (review question 1). (b) The Iberian sale name and the EU farming term are attributes of the same kind: they grade the same product and fix no different composition (JC4(d)); none of the J1 sale names is a kind. **OWNER:** review question 5 (ladders are attributes; only a name that fixes a different composition or process is a kind). (c) Retailer labels are `platform_tag` entries, not tiers (C4(c)). (d) Designations (Kobe Beef, Prosciutto di Parma, the German protected-name meats listed in the Leitsätze annex [S17]) go in a `designation` table: `designation_id`, name, `scheme` (PDO, GI, national), `register_id`, `authority` (the consortium or association, never a maker), `kinds_served`, rule summary, date, sources. Placement (JC1(c)): a designation that binds one kind is a non-maker named form of it carrying `designation_id` (Prosciutto di Parma under dry-cured ham); one that spans several kinds is a tag (Kobe Beef); a designation is a kind only under C11 (no J1 case). **OWNER:** review questions 1 and 2 (adopt JC1; written exception for wine and spirit names, which does not touch J1). (e) Tier entries (`subject_id`, `scheme`, `value`, `source_url`, `date`) are used only for maker lines whose tier a source states, for example a producer's own named grade lines once a source states them; they never go on a kind and a named form does not inherit a maker's tier. A tier scheme enters only with one fetched source and preferably a second from another region (C4(e)): the Snake River breed price tiers [S24] and the Arcecarne grades [S20] each rest on one page and are not entered as tiers until a second source is fetched †.

**Cross-scheme comparison is not valid.** Sources say an A5 and a "1++" or a USDA Prime are not equivalent [S6][S22]. Store each standard separately; do not store a "same as" table. A grade is assigned to the carcass [S2][S31][S22]; a retail product carries only the claim of its source carcass, which is not stored (C16).

**Price band (JC2).** One unit band for the J domain: `unit_price_band` in USD per kilogram, from a dated, sourced list price, with the bands set at the first data load (open question 4). **OWNER:** review question 3 (this replaces the C4(d) item band for J1). Per-kilo spread in the pages fetched, in the source currency: UK lean mince about £10.40 per kg and pork belly slices £9.00 per kg, Parma ham £37.50 to £57.14 per kg, and a 35-day-aged sirloin £85.71 per kg [S35][S36]; a 7.1 kg bellota ham for €389 is about €55 per kg (my arithmetic) [S28]. ## 6. Makers and brands

**Recommended maker centrality: identity, led by origin and scheme, not by the manufacturer name.** Buyers of premium meat seek a ranch, a farm system, an appellation or a ham house: Crowd Cow names the ranch on each product [S13], Snake River Farms sells purebred Kurobuta pork under its own name [S24] (its "Gold" and "Black" beef line names were seen in search excerpts only †), D'Artagnan sells under one brand [S12], Hawksmoor and Finnebrogue appear as maker names on UK lines [S35][S36], and the Japanese wholesaler lists prefecture-branded pork and chicken [S14]. For commodity cuts the maker is incidental. No platform dependence (no compatibility ledger, C13).

**Maker ledger.** Recommended, with the C1 core columns, because a quality-led buyer looks up the producer. `maker_class` keeps its C1 values (an appellation body or association is not a maker; it is the `authority` of a designation row, and "producer (farm or ranch)" is a supply role, not an identity class). One J extension column, `supply_role` (multi-valued, JC3 list): farm or ranch; processor or packer; curer or smokehouse; importer or trader; retail own-label. **OWNER:** review question 4 (adopt the single `supply_role` column; JC3 has no value for a butcher, which I read as processor or packer). Not maker columns: `farm_system` (grass-fed, pasture-raised, free range, organic are product attributes, section 3), `certifications` (attributes of the named form or product) and `scheme_ids` (derived from the named-form ledger). A bottled-by or packed-by fact on an own-label line uses C1's `built_by_maker_id`. Known-for named forms are not a ledger column (C1): derive them from the named-form ledger.

**Examples seen** (supply roles are my reading of each source's own description): ranches Little Belt Cattle Co. and Royal Ranch Farms [S13] (farm or ranch); Snake River Farms [S24] (farm or ranch); Canvin & Gunner, The Butcher's Board, Hawksmoor [S36] (processor or packer); M&S "Heritage Gold" (retail own-label) [S35]; Finnebrogue, Golfera, Unearthed [S35] (processor or packer); Maximiliano Jabugo (ham house: curer or smokehouse) [S28]. The Consorzio del Prosciutto di Parma and the Kobe Beef Marketing and Distribution Promotion Association are designation authorities, not makers [S33][S34][S31].

**Kinds, named forms and makers (C2).** A named form is maker-bound only for a maker line (a "Gold" ribeye filet; Finnebrogue Naked sausage). A cut name (tomahawk), a regional name (entrecôte) and a protected name (Prosciutto di Parma) are `maker_bound: no`; the protected name also carries a `designation_id`. A line that spans several kinds is a C1 line row; a line inside one kind is a maker-bound named form.

## 8. Order inside a leaf

**Axis: canonical form first, then position on the animal.** For J1-A leaves: whole-muscle steak and roast cuts first, ordered **front to back along the carcass** (chuck, rib, loin, round, then belly-side and lower cuts), then kinds sold whole (suckling pig), then primals or sides (open question 11), then mince, then shaped raw mince, then offal last. Within the same position, steak cuts come before roasting joints. The tree of section 3 follows this order (beef: chuck roast, flat iron, ribeye, striploin, tenderloin, T-bone, top sirloin, rump cap, skirt, short rib, brisket, shin, then ground beef, shaped raw mince and tongue; pork: jowl, collar, loin chop, tenderloin, ribs, belly, hock, trotter, suckling pig, ground pork, shaped raw mince). Reason: every cut catalogue found is built in carcass order: the MAFF guide numbers chuck roll to knuckle [S5], Grassland's eight primals run chuck, rib, loin, round, flank, plate, brisket, shank [S25], Interbev has separate forequarter and hindquarter posters [S7], and the Japanese guide teaches four blocks (back, shoulder, belly, rump and leg) [S16]. Leaves are ordered by animal as printed (cattle, pigs, sheep and goats, poultry, then game, then other meats). For J1-B: by cure class as in section 3, then, within a leaf, the canonical dry-cured ham first and the other pieces front to back (jowl, neck, shoulder, loin, belly, then cured beef). For J1-C: by process class (fresh, dry, scalded, cooked), then the canonical form of the class first. For J1-D: plain-cooked, then tinned and potted, then pâtés and terrines. Named forms: C7 (maker order, then first-introduction year, then alphabetical); a non-maker-bound name with no attested year goes alphabetically.

## 10. Recommendation and open questions

**Recommended structure: Candidate B.** J1 → sub-family (**how the meat is prepared for sale**: fresh & frozen; cured, smoked & dried; sausages; cooked, tinned & potted) → leaf (**the animal** in J1-A; **cure class** in J1-B; **process class** in J1-C; none in J1-D) → kind (**the named retail cut** in J1-A where three cuts are attested, otherwise "<species>, whole or jointed"; the type of cured piece, sausage or product elsewhere) → named form (national and sub-cut names, protected names that bind one kind, varieties, maker lines). **Maximum depth: four levels below the category** (three in J1-D).
