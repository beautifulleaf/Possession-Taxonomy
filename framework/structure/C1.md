## 0. Category

**Code and name:** C1 Tools & Workshop Equipment.

**Membership test** (CATEGORIES_v0_6, step 32, branch 1): *a tool, instrument or machine made to cut, shape, join, measure, test, lift, power or repair: hand and power tools, workshop machines, ladders, building and trade measuring and testing tools, portable generators, pumps, welding and soldering equipment, pocket knives and multi-tools, sack trucks, dollies and hand carts, tool boxes, tool bags and tool storage.* Category list line: "hand and power tools, workshop machines, ladders, measuring and testing tools for building and trades, generators, tool storage."

**Notes and precedence sentences that name C1:**
- Step 10 (E4 Weapons): machetes and axes made for cutting wood or vegetation are C1 or C2; kitchen and utility knives are B5 and C1.
- Step 11 (G1 Vehicles): hand carts, sack trucks and dollies are C1; wheelbarrows and garden carts are C2; shopping trolleys are A4.
- Step 17 (A4 Bags): tool bags are C1.
- Step 23 (C6 Optical & Scientific): a measuring tool made for a task goes with that task; building and trade measuring tools are C1; rulers and postal scales C5; sewing tape measures C4; pool and soil test kits C2; kitchen scales B5.
- Step 28 (C4 Craft): tools for shaping wood, metal and stone, and for electronics work, are C1, whoever uses them.
- Step 30 (B1 Furniture & Storage): tool boxes and tool storage are C1.
- Step 29 (C2 Garden): mowers, trimmers, leaf blowers, pressure washers, wheelbarrows are C2 and are tried before step 32.
- S3 (parts follow the whole): a component, attachment or consumable made to be fitted to or used up in one kind of object goes to that object's category. Tool accessories (bits, blades, batteries made for tools) therefore stay in C1; "household batteries" are C8; fuels are C8; cleaning products are B6 whatever they are used in.
- Step 32 branch 2 (C8): fasteners, hinges, fittings, plumbing and electrical supplies, extension leads, lubricants, fuels, packing materials are C8, not C1.

"Kits & Twinpacks" and "Bare Units" are sales formats listed as categories. Cainz lists electric driver/drill, grinder, sander, cutter, chainsaw, trimmer/router/planer, jigsaw/circular saw, mixer, blower/dust collector, welder, compressor/air tools, pump, heater, radio and rechargeable battery, with brand shortcuts (Makita, HiKOKI, Bosch, Karcher, Ryobi); voltages (7.2, 10.8, 14.4, 18 V) appear in product names.

The platform is a retail facet in its own right. The rest of the Home Depot tool tree could not be read.
- **Workshop machinery, US and UK** [S16, S17]: Grizzly splits Woodworking and Metalworking first, then machine type (Table Saws, Bandsaws, Planers, Jointers, Drill Presses, Lathes, Mortising, Scroll Saws; for metal: Bandsaws, Lathes, Cold Cut Saws, Mills, Presses, Welding & Plasma), and below that a construction class: table saws as Jobsite, Hybrid, Cabinet, Sliding Table, Rip; planers, drill presses, lathes as Benchtop versus Stationary or Floor. Axminster's machinery has Saws, Planers & Thicknessers, Sanders, Drills & Morticers, Lathes, Dust Extractors, Bench Grinders/Polishers/Linishers, Sharpening Systems, Spindle Moulders & Tenoners, CNC, Powerfeeds, and further divides lathes into Engineering and Woodturning and planers into Planer Thicknessers and Thicknessers [S17, S19].
- **Ladders and storage, UK** [S30, S32]: Screwfix step ladders are described by form (swingback A-frame, platform, combination), material (aluminium, fibreglass), height, tread count and brand. Screwfix toolboxes are Plastic, Wheeled, Metal, with a "Compatible Stacking System" facet (TSTAK, ToughSystem, PACKOUT, BUNKER Load-Up, Connecx, E-Case).
- **Generators and welders, US** [S33, S34]: a generator seller splits by Generator Type (inverter, conventional portable), Fuel Type (gas, dual fuel, tri fuel) and Usage (home backup, outdoors/RV, jobsite); a welder buying guide splits by process (TIG, MIG, stick, multi-process) and then amperage and supply voltage.

**Enthusiast communities and reference sites.**
- **Pro Tool Reviews (US)** [S8]: ranks cordless drills in a scored head-to-head, labels classes Pro, "DIY/Prosumer" and DIY, and lists brands by user type (Prosumer/Serious DIY: Craftsman, Masterforce, Ryobi, Skil; DIY: Black and Decker, Greenworks, HART, Worx). Its main test set is the pro-grade hammer drill; compact hammer drills and plain drill/drivers appear in a separate "more cordless drills we recommend" list as a different class.
- **CordlessPicker (US)** [S7]: a table of 13 battery platforms, each tagged Professional (DeWalt 20V MAX, Makita 18V LXT, Milwaukee M18, Flex 24V), Prosumer (Bosch 18V, Metabo HPT MultiVolt, Ridgid 18V), DIY & homeowner (Ryobi ONE+, Craftsman V20, Kobalt 24V) or Budget (Hart 20V), with an ecosystem size. Its own text says "the battery decides everything downstream".
- **Highland Woodworking (US)** [S22]: planes grouped by role and length (smoothers = Stanley 1 to 4-1/2, jacks = 5, 5-1/2 and 6, jointers = 6, 7 and 8), and buyers choose between a refurbished antique Stanley and a premium modern plane such as Lie-Nielsen.
- **Machine Atlas (UK)** [S18]: lists machines by maker and type; its Axminster entry records the maker's own ladder Craft/Hobby, Trade, Industrial.

**Collectors and the resale market.**
- **Wikipedia, Antique tool** [S20]: collectors organise by maker (Stanley, Disston, Norris, Miller's Falls), by type (hammers, braces, axes, saws, patented and transitional planes), by period, by place, by occupation (cooper, machinist, watchmaker) and by "type study" of a single model (Stanley #6 jointer, Norris A5 smoother). It names the Mid-West Tool Collectors Association, the Early American Industries Association, the Tools and Trades History Society and the Hand Tool Preservation Association of Australia. Maker, period and place are collector axes, not tool-type axes.
- **Japanese hand-tool dealers** [S9, S10, S11]: Japamart groups by type (Kanna planes, Nomi chisels, Nokogiri saws, Genno hammers, Marking & Measuring, Toishi whetstones, Specialty) and offers separate browse routes by brand, by steel (White #1/#2, Blue #1/#2, Aogami Super, Swedish, tamahagane, HSS) and by forging region (Sanjo, Echigo Yoita, Banshu Miki, Ono, Kyoto). Hida Tool's main facet is a brand list of about 60 Japanese makers and it lists named products such as the Gyokucho #661–#667 ryoba saws. Suzuki-ya divides chisels first by use (striking versus push/paring), then lists traditional sub-types (oire-nomi bench, chu-tataki-nomi, atsu-nomi, tateguya mortise; usu-nomi, shinogi-nomi, yama-ari-nomi dovetail) and then by named smith (Ohuchi, Takamichi, Fujikawa Yasaku).

**Standards bodies and trade classifications.**
- **UNSPSC** [S13]: segment 27 Tools and General Machinery; family 27110000 Hand tools has 19 classes by action or job: cutting/crimping/punching, forming, wrenches and drivers, measuring and layout, rough and finishing, agriculture/forestry/garden, holding and clamping, masonry and concrete, marking, fastener setting, prying and bending, sealing, power tools, tool attachments and accessories, dispensing, brushes, pulling, tool kits, precision hand tools. The sibling families are hydraulic, pneumatic and automotive specialty. UNSPSC therefore puts power tools inside "hand tools" as one class and keeps accessories and kits as classes.
- **Harmonized System, chapter 82 and heading 8467** [S14, S15]: chapter 82 covers non-powered hand tools by action (8201 agricultural hand tools; 8202 saws; 8203 files, pliers, cutters, punches; 8204 spanners and wrenches; 8205 other hand tools, vices, clamps, anvils; 8206 sets; 8207 interchangeable tools; 8208 machine knives). Powered hand tools go to heading 8467, which the EU Combined Nomenclature splits first by energy (pneumatic; self-contained electric motor; other motor) and then by action (drills; saws, with chainsaws and circular saws; grinders and sanders; planers; hedge trimmers), with parts separate. Customs thus separates hand from powered, keeps accessories apart, and divides powered tools by energy first.
- **Battery alliances** [S5, S6, S25]: the Cordless Alliance System (over 50 brands, 500+ tools, "100% compatibility in the 18V class") and Bosch's Power for All and AMPShare alliances show that a battery platform spans several makers; Bosch states that its professional 18V system and its DIY system "will not be unified" because professionals and DIYers have different requirements.

**Museums and reference works.**
- **Japan House London** [S12]: a 1943 survey of carpenters in Ōta Ward, Tokyo, found 179 tool types needed to build a full-quality building (72 for cheaper construction); a 1985 survey found only 20 types in frequent use and 59 occasional. The essay groups tools by job in construction order: axes (kiri-ono, wari-ono, masakiri), marking (sumi-kake, kebiki), saws (nokogiri), planes (kanna, awase-kanna), hammers (genno: ryōguchi, funate, kataguchi) and chisels (striking versus paring).
- **Wikipedia, Hand tool** [S21]: general categories attributed to the American Industrial Hygiene Association are wrenches, pliers, cutters, striking tools, struck or hammered tools, screwdrivers, vises, clamps, snips, saws, drills and knives; portable power tools are excluded.

**Where the sources agree.** Every retailer and every classification separates (a) hand tools, (b) powered hand tools, (c) stationary or bench machinery and (d) accessories and consumables. Below that, the stable axis is the job the tool does (saw, drill, grind, plane, fasten, clamp, measure). Measuring tools are the most ambiguous item: Bunnings, UNSPSC and Screwfix keep them under hand tools; Bosch, Starrett and Mitutoyo run them as a separate product family [S24, S28, S29].

**Where they split the same objects differently.** (1) Screwfix and Axminster mix trade or activity groups (plumbing, tiling, woodturning, metal working) with action groups [S3, S19]. (2) Customs splits powered tools by energy (pneumatic, electric, other) where retailers split by action and treat cordless versus corded as a filter [S14, S23]. (3) Retailers treat the battery platform as a facet or a shopping route [S23, S26, S35]; no classification treats it as a tool type. (4) Retail trees file pressure washers, compressors, chainsaws, worklights and radios under power tools [S1, S27], while the framework key sends pressure washers to C2. (5) Mitutoyo and Starrett sell precision gauging to machinists and quality labs [S28, S29], a different buyer from the tape-measure and level buyer.

## 2. The first axis people choose by

**Evidence on what owners and buyers decide first.**
1. **Mechanism class: hand, powered hand-held, or machine.** It is the first cut at Bunnings [S1], Axminster (Hand Tools / Power Tools / Machinery / Accessories) [S19], Hornbach [S4], Grizzly (Woodworking / Metalworking machinery) [S16], the UN classification [S13] and the customs schedule [S14, S15]. Wikipedia's definition of a hand tool excludes portable power tools [S21].
2. **For powered tools, the battery platform comes before the tool.** Home Depot gives Battery Platform its own filter [S23]; CordlessPicker states that "the battery decides everything downstream" [S7]; Bosch lets owners "buy bare tools" because the system is compatible back to 2008 [S25]; Komeri and OBI sell tools solo and sell their own battery-sharing series [S26, S35]. The questions buyers ask are therefore "which platform" and "pro or DIY grade", followed by the job.
3. **Then the job (action).** Every tree below the first cut divides by the work done: drilling, sawing, grinding and sanding, routing and planing, fastening, gripping, striking [S1, S2, S13, S15, S26, S27].
4. **Then the grade.** Bosch blue versus green [S24], Komeri's "pro" and "DIY" leaf labels [S26], Axminster's Occasional/Regular/Frequent use rating [S19], and Pro Tool Reviews' Pro versus DIY/Prosumer classes [S8] all place the grade after the tool type.
5. **For hand tools in the premium and collector market, the maker and the material come before the finer tool type.** Hida Tool's main facet is brand [S10]; Japamart has parallel browse routes by brand, steel and forging region beside tool type [S9]; collectors choose maker, period or place [S20].

**Conclusion for the first axis:** the primary axis is the mechanism class (how the tool is driven and mounted), then the work action. Platform, grade, maker, steel and trade are second-order and belong on the tool as attributes, ledgers or generated views.

## 3. Candidate structures

Common to all three: the category has four levels below it at most (sub-family, leaf, kind, named form). Power source, voltage, battery platform, brand, grade and trade are never levels.

### Candidate A (recommended): equipment class → work action → tool type → named form

| Level | Stated axis | What it holds |
|---|---|---|
| Sub-family | How the equipment is driven, mounted and used (hand, held-and-powered, bench/stationary machine, process or power equipment, measuring, access and handling, storage, accessories) | 8 sub-families |
| Leaf | The work done (cut, plane and chisel, strike, fasten, grip, drill and drive, grind and sand...) or, for non-tool sub-families, the thing measured, reached or carried | about 45 leaves |
| Kind | Generic tool type a person owns one of (claw hammer, impact driver, jobsite table saw) | about 400–500 kinds (estimate, section 4) |
| Named form | Model, series or traditional pattern (Stanley No. 4, Flex FX1371T, ryoba) | open-ended |

Evidence for the sub-family axis: Bunnings, Axminster, Hornbach, Grizzly, UNSPSC and the customs schedule all make the hand / powered / machine / accessory cut first [S1, S4, S13–S17, S19]. Evidence for the leaf axis: Bunnings, Screwfix, Cainz, Komeri, UNSPSC and HS all divide below that by the work done [S1, S2, S13, S15, S26, S27].

**Where Candidate A breaks.**
1. *Angle grinder and chainsaw*: one tool, two actions (grinding and cutting); retailers put angle grinders under both Grinders and Saws [S1, S2]. Rule: the primary designed action. Chainsaws also fall to C2 or forestry (section 9).
2. *Mitre saw and tile saw*: motor-driven, bench-mounted but carried to site; Komeri lists the sliding mitre saw beside hand-held circular saws [S26], Grizzly sells jobsite table saws as machines [S16]. The sub-family boundary between "held" and "bench" needs a stated test (does the operator support it in the hand while it cuts).
3. *Combination machines* (planer-thicknesser, combination lathe-mill, wood-and-metal bandsaw) [S16, S17] and *multi-process welders* [S34]: one object, several kinds.
4. *Pneumatic and hydraulic tools*: customs makes energy the first split [S14] and UNSPSC keeps pneumatic and hydraulic as separate families [S13]; under Candidate A they are kinds that carry the energy attribute (air impact wrench with the impact wrench kind).

### Candidate B: work action first, across hand and powered tools

| Level | Stated axis |
|---|---|
| Sub-family | The work done (cutting, drilling and driving, shaping and smoothing, fastening, gripping, striking, joining, measuring, lifting) |
| Leaf | Tool type group (saws; planes; chisels) |
| Kind | Generic type, with hand, powered and machine versions as separate kinds (panel saw, circular saw, table saw) |
| Named form | Model or pattern |

Example sub-families and leaves (illustrative, 12 kinds): *Sawing* → hand saws (panel saw, pull saw), powered saws (circular saw, jigsaw), machine saws (cabinet table saw, bandsaw); *Smoothing* → planes (smoothing plane, kanna), powered planers (electric hand planer), machine surfacers (jointer, thicknesser); *Drilling* → hand drills (brace), drill/driver, drill press. This matches UNSPSC (classes by job, with power tools as one class) [S13], Komeri's function route [S26] and the collector habit of comparing a hand plane with a power planer [S17: Axminster's advice list includes "Making the Leap from Hand Tools to Machines"]. Where it breaks:
1. *Cordless versus corded versions of one tool* sit together by action but buyers shop them by platform [S23].
2. *Oscillating multi-tools, rotary tools and heat guns* have no single action.
3. *Generators, compressors, ladders, tool boxes* have no work action and need a catch-all sub-family anyway.
4. *Machine-only items* (lathe, mill, press) have no hand equivalent, so the "same action across mechanisms" benefit vanishes for a third of the category.

### Candidate C: trade or activity first (Screwfix, Axminster "Activities", Werner project types)

| Level | Stated axis |
|---|---|
| Sub-family | The trade or activity (woodworking, metalworking, plumbing, electrical, tiling, masonry, painting, woodturning) |
| Leaf | Tool group within the trade |
| Kind | Generic tool |

Example (10 kinds): *Plumbing* → pipe cutter, basin wrench, press tool; *Electrical* → wire stripper, cable puller, voltage tester; *Tiling* → tile cutter, notched trowel; *Woodworking* → chisel, plane, mortise machine. This is how Screwfix's hand-tool page [S3], Axminster's activity navigation [S19] and Werner's project-type navigation [S31] are laid out. Where it breaks: pliers, screwdrivers, hammers, tape measures and clamps belong to every trade; the framework already treats setting and user as modifiers (Appendix A); and C4 (craft) and C2 (garden) claim some of the same trades. Recommended use: a generated view (see section 7), not a level.

## 4. Depth and size

**Rough count of generic kinds (my estimate, built from the sources below): 400–500.**
- Hand tools about 150. Basis: UNSPSC lists 19 classes of hand tools [S13]; HS chapter 82 names about 8 hand-tool headings, each covering a dozen or more tool types [S15]; the Japan House survey counted 179 distinct tool types for one trade, carpentry, in 1943 and 72 for a cheaper building [S12]. Western hand tools overlap with that count only in part, so 150 generic kinds across all trades is a cautious figure.
- Hand-held power tools about 60. Basis: Screwfix lists about 20 power-tool categories and Bunnings 17 [S1, S2]; the EU nomenclature has about 10 powered hand-tool subheadings [S14]; Komeri adds the pro and DIY and voltage variants of the same types, which collapse into one kind each [S26].
- Workshop machines about 60. Basis: Grizzly's menus show 20 woodworking machine types and 17 metalworking machine types [S16], Axminster lists 11 machinery categories [S17], and the construction classes that buyers compare (jobsite, hybrid, cabinet, sliding table; engineering versus woodturning lathe) multiply some of those types [S16, S17].
- Welding, soldering and site power about 30; measuring, layout and testing about 50 (Mitutoyo sells 5,500+ instruments in seven catalogue sections, of which handheld small tools are one [S29]; Starrett lists nine precision-measuring families [S28]); access, lifting and handling about 20; tool storage about 25; accessories, attachments and consumables about 80 (UNSPSC has a separate class, HS a separate heading 8207 [S13, S15]).

**Rough count of named forms: 1,500–3,000 worth recording (estimate).** Retail volumes are far higher (Cainz 29,600 power-tool listings [S27]; Hornbach 9,911 hand-tool results [S4]), but most listings are sizes, kits and colours of the same design. Named forms worth recording are the traditional patterns (Stanley numbers 1–8 and half sizes [S22]; Japanese saw, plane, chisel and hammer patterns [S11, S12]; ladder forms [S30]), the battery platforms and stacking systems [S7, S32], and the series or models that review sites rank [S8].

**Where depth concentrates.**
1. *Hand tools in the premium and collector market* (planes, chisels, saws): the Stanley number series, the Japanese sub-types [S11, S12, S22] and collector type studies [S20].
2. *Hand-held power tools*: shallow in kinds but deep in forms and attributes (platform, voltage, brushless, kit or bare) [S2, S7, S23].
3. *Workshop machines*: construction class matters (jobsite, hybrid, cabinet, sliding) [S16].
4. *Precision measuring*: many instrument types and accuracy grades [S28, S29].

**Broad, medium and narrow leaves (estimate).**
- Broad: striking and prying, turning and fastening, gripping and clamping (hand); drilling and driving, sawing and cutting (power); accessories; length and level.
- Medium: planing, chiselling and carving; filing and sharpening; grinding and sanding; sawing machines; welding; ladders; toolboxes and cases.
- Narrow: routing and planing (power), turning machines, drilling machines, hand trucks, precision gauging, marking and layout, site power and air.

## 4b. Choice level

**Rule applied:** a kind stops at the level where buyers actually compare quality or makers. Finer distinctions below that are named forms or attributes.

**Recommended stop for C1: the tool type defined by what it does to the work, with three exceptions that go one step finer and two that stay coarser.**

1. **Hand tools: tool type, split wherever the working action differs.**
   - Evidence for splitting: planes are bought by role and length (smoother, jack, jointer), not as one "plane" [S22]; chisels divide into striking and push (paring) types with different construction [S11, S12]; the Japanese pull saws are sold as separate types: the fine dozuki for joinery, the double-edged ryoba and the flush-cutting azebiki [S9]. Each of these is a kind; the Stanley numbers, the Norris A5 and the Gyokucho model numbers are named forms [S10, S20, S22].
   - Evidence for stopping: size, steel, handle, finish and tooth count are filters on one type, not types: Bunnings and Screwfix leaves are the type (Hammers, Pliers, Screwdrivers) [S1, S3], Hornbach offers brand as a filter on its 9,911 hand-tool results [S4], and Screwfix filters ladders by height, material, tread count and brand [S30]. Japanese dealers list steel (White #2, Blue #1, Aogami Super, Swedish, HSS) and forging region as separate browse routes next to tool type [S9], so steel is an attribute.
2. **Hand-held power tools: the action mechanism, because buyers compare within it.**
   - Drill/driver, hammer drill, impact driver, rotary hammer and impact wrench are kept apart by Screwfix (Drills versus Impact Drivers & Wrenches) [S2], by Komeri (impact driver, vibration drill, hammer drill, impact wrench) [S26] and by Pro Tool Reviews, whose head-to-head is the hammer drill [S8]. Each is a kind.
   - Voltage class, brushless motor, corded versus cordless, battery platform, kit versus bare unit and DIY/prosumer/pro grade are attributes: Screwfix sells "Bare Units" and "Kits & Twinpacks" as formats of the same tool [S2], Home Depot filters on platform and voltage [S23], Komeri repeats "18V pro / 36V pro / AC pro / DIY" across every action group [S26], Bosch sells blue and green versions of one kind of drill [S24]. Because the same grade labels repeat under every kind, they are modifiers.
3. **Workshop machines: go one step deeper where the construction class changes the use** (exception to the tool-type rule).
   - Grizzly divides table saws into Jobsite, Hybrid, Cabinet, Sliding Table and Rip [S16]; Axminster divides lathes into Engineering (metal) and Woodturning, and planers into Planer Thicknessers and Thicknessers [S17]. Each is a kind. Benchtop versus stationary appears under planers, drill presses, lathes, mortisers and scroll saws [S16]; where it repeats across types it is a size-class attribute, not a kind.
4. **Measuring tools: instrument type** (tape, level, laser distance measurer, caliper, micrometer, indicator). Accuracy grade, resolution and range are attributes; makers such as Starrett and Mitutoyo are compared by line and tolerance [S28, S29].
5. **Ladders: ladder form** (step, platform step, extension, telescopic, combination), the facets Screwfix and Werner use [S30, S31]; material (aluminium, fibreglass) and height band are attributes (material is a modifier under Appendix A unless it changes the form).
6. **Accessories: accessory type** (drill bit, saw blade, abrasive disc, battery pack, charger). Bit diameter, shank, tooth count and grit are attributes. The battery pack is a kind whose platform is a compatibility attribute [S23, S25].
7. **Tool storage: container type** (toolbox, wheeled toolbox, tool bag, stackable case); the stacking system (PACKOUT, TSTAK, ToughSystem and others) is a compatibility attribute [S32].

**Exceptions that stay coarser.** Hand-tool families that retailers treat as one leaf with many size variants (spanners and sockets, hex keys, clamps) should be one kind per recognisable type, with size, drive and finish as attributes. Types that buyers compare separately (combination spanner, ratchet spanner, adjustable wrench) are separate kinds [S1, S15]. Measuring-tape length, clamp capacity, bit diameter and sheet size are never kinds.

**Where the level differs inside C1.** Hand tools and machines are finer than hand-held power tools because quality differences sit in the type itself (a Japanese chisel versus a Western bench chisel; a cabinet saw versus a jobsite saw). For power tools quality sits in the platform, grade and motor spec, which are attributes.

## 5. Quality and tier

**Choice: consensus-ranked, with a spec layer underneath.** No fetched page shows a formal standard that ranks tools from DIY to professional. The ordering in use is set by makers, retailers and review sites, and each maps to measurable specs. (Safety and performance standards for power tools, for example EN or IEC test standards, exist but no page in this batch was fetched to attest them; they are left for stage 3 sourcing.)

**The orderings found.**
1. **Duty grade (consensus, three or four steps).** Axminster rates machines Occasional use (light DIY, lower power, lighter build), Regular use (dedicated hobbyist or small professional workshop) and Frequent use (daily professional use, "not industrial grade"), by "how frequently and for how long the machine is expected to be used", with type of work, user and manufacturer also considered [S19]. Machine Atlas records the same maker's ladder as Craft/Hobby, Trade, Industrial [S18]. Bosch splits professional (blue: daily use, multi-day jobs, extended warranty) from DIY (green: occasional use, short jobs), and attaches specs (GSR 18-2-LI Plus 63 Nm and 1,900 rpm versus PSR 18 LI-2 46 Nm and 1,340 rpm) [S24]. Komeri's leaf labels say pro or DIY and 18V or 36V or AC [S26]. Pro Tool Reviews uses Pro, Prosumer/Serious DIY and DIY, with brand lists per class [S8]. CordlessPicker labels each platform Professional, Prosumer, DIY & homeowner or Budget [S7].
2. **Spec rank (numeric).** Voltage class (7.2 V, 10.8 V, 14.4 V, 18 V, 36 V and over in Japanese listings [S26, S27]; 12 V versus 18 V systems at Bosch [S25]); torque in N·m, speed, hammer rate and weight [S8, S24]; motor horsepower and duty cycle on machines [S16]; amperage on welders [S34]; maximum static load and height on ladders [S30]; resolution and range on gauges [S28, S29].
3. **Material rank for hand tools (consensus).** Japanese dealers rank blade steel in a stated order: White #2 and #1, Blue #2 and #1, Aogami Super, Swedish steel, tamahagane, HSS [S9]; forging region and named smith add prestige [S9, S11]. Collectors rank by maker, rarity and condition [S20].
4. **Score-based ranking.** Pro Tool Reviews scores drills out of 100 on standardized tests [S8].

**How it appears in the structure.** As a modifier on the named form (a model), never a level:
- *Grade*: DIY / prosumer / professional / industrial, mapped from the maker's own label (Bosch green and blue; Axminster Occasional, Regular, Frequent; Komeri pro and DIY) with the source stated per row. The vocabulary (three or four values) is an owner decision.
- *Spec attributes* with units: voltage class, torque, duty cycle, motor power, steel grade, load rating, accuracy.
- *Platform tier*: the platform ledger records the review-site tier (Professional, Prosumer, DIY, Budget) [S7].
- Some kinds exist only at the professional grade (for example construction-site machines); for those the grade is a constant, not a filter.

## 6. Makers and brands

**Choice: platform.** For hand-held power tools, tool batteries and tool storage, the maker sets compatibility: a battery fits only its own platform or an alliance of platforms, and a stacking case fits only its own system.
- *Evidence*: Home Depot gives Battery Platform its own filter [S23]; Bosch promises 18V compatibility back to 2008 (professional) and 2007 (DIY) and sells bare tools without batteries [S6, S25]; the Cordless Alliance System lists "over 50 brands" and "500+ tools" on one 18V battery, and Bosch's Power for All and AMPShare alliances do the same on separate systems [S5, S6, S25]; Komeri sells its own battery-sharing B-Share series and HiKOKI's MultiVolt batteries as products in their own right [S26]; Screwfix filters toolboxes by "Compatible Stacking System" (PACKOUT, TSTAK, ToughSystem, BUNKER Load-Up, Connecx, E-Case) [S32]; OBI sells store-brand and maker battery systems under named families (Power X-Change, 1 PowerSystem) [S35].
- *Identity applies in the premium hand-tool market*: Hida Tool's main facet is the maker (about 60 Japanese makers) [S10]; Japamart has separate browse routes by brand, steel and forging region [S9]; Suzuki-ya sorts chisels by named smith [S11]; collectors choose by maker (Stanley, Disston, Norris, Miller's Falls) [S20]; Highland points buyers to Lie-Nielsen as the premium alternative to refurbished Stanley [S22]; Starrett and Mitutoyo market their names as standards of precision [S28, S29].
- *Makers are incidental for commodity hand tools and consumables* (hammers, pliers, drill bits): the retailer trees treat them as filters [S1, S3, S4].

**Recommendation: a maker ledger plus a platform ledger, kept as tables, never as levels.**
- *Maker ledger* rows: maker; regional names and owner (flag for regional duplicates: Komeri lists Ryobi under Kyocera [S26] while US sources list Ryobi ONE+ as a DIY platform [S7, S23]; both pages name a MultiVolt line, HiKOKI in Japan [S26] and Metabo HPT in the US [S7]; the corporate relationships were not verified in a fetched page and must be attested before any row says they are the same maker); known-for kinds; grade lines (Bosch blue and green, Axminster Craft/Hobby, Trade, Industrial, Engineer series [S18, S24]); platforms joined; scene tags (Japanese forged hand tools, woodworking, metalworking, trade); tier; price band; country; era; each row attested.
- *Platform ledger* rows: platform name; nominal and marketed voltage (for example "20V MAX" versus "18V" labels in [S7]); makers who share it; alliance (CAS, Power for All, AMPShare) [S5, S6, S25]; backward-compatibility years [S6, S25]; sibling platforms of the same maker (Milwaukee M12 versus M18; DeWalt 20V MAX versus 60V FLEXVOLT [S23]; Bosch 12V versus 18V [S25]); review-site tier [S7]. Tools, batteries and chargers carry a platform attribute pointing here.
- *Storage-system ledger* rows: PACKOUT, TSTAK, ToughSystem, BUNKER Load-Up, Connecx, E-Case [S32].

## 8. Order inside a leaf

**Recommended axis: canonical form first, then spec order (size or capacity ascending); named forms by series number, then maker.**
- *Reason*: the sources already order by role then size. Highland orders planes by the Stanley number from 1 to 8, which tracks length and role (smoothers, jacks, jointers) [S22]; Axminster orders machines by duty class Occasional → Regular → Frequent [S19]; Grizzly orders benchtop before stationary and jobsite before cabinet [S16]; Screwfix and Komeri order power-tool kinds by action and voltage class [S2, S26].
- *Leaf order inside a sub-family*: by stage of the job (mark and measure → cut → shape and smooth → join and fasten → grip → finish), which is the order in which the Japan House essay presents carpenters' tools (axes, marking, saws, planes, hammers and chisels) [S12]. Alphabetical order is the fallback.
- *Named forms in a collector kind*: by series number or chronology (Stanley number, then type or issue) because collectors work by type study [S20, S22].
- *Not by price or popularity*: the sources use "top sellers" and price sorts as views [S3, S30], which belong to generated pages.
